# Critical care: hemodynamics and acid-base language

## Source status

CC-01 supplies a hemodynamic-monitoring discussion in a COVID-era context. CC-02 supplies an extensive acid-base conversation with cases, a speaker-developed framework, alternative views, and several heuristics. This profile uses their terminology and teaching structure, not their treatment recommendations as clinical authority.

## Hemodynamics

**Source-anchored vocabulary:** hemodynamic monitoring; blood pressure; perfusion; intravascular volume; cardiac output; stroke volume; fluid responsiveness; fluid overload; fluid balance; arterial waveform analysis; vasoplegic shock; vasopressor; passive leg raise; bedside ultrasound.

Anchors: CC-01, 1:13–2:41, lines 129–261; 6:15–6:46, 617–661; 7:12–8:21, 705–825; 10:16–11:56, 1013–1169; 14:38–15:00, 1437–1481.

### Preserve the clinical question

Use the clinical measurement and its interpretation instead of an unexplained dashboard word. But do not equate a displayed blood pressure with organ perfusion or a suggested fluid response with clinical benefit.

The source explicitly asks both whether a patient responds to a bolus and whether that bolus will help or harm. Keep those as separate questions; do not make “fluid responsive” a synonym for “needs fluid.” CC-01, 6:28–6:46, 633–661.

The transcript's “fill, flow, pressure” phrase is a heuristic attributed to its speaker. Do not encode a universal resuscitation sequence from that phrase. In a module already describing preload, output, or pressure, use the established term; if the referent of “filling” is unclear, flag rather than substituting a specific measured variable.

### Labels and bedside voice

Illustrations below are authored, not direct source or repository quotations:

| Less useful                                    | Better when the context is already established                                                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| “The flow number is down.”                     | “The estimated cardiac output has decreased.”                                                                         |
| “Give volume to improve the support story.”    | State the original, source-supported question or action about a fluid bolus; do not invent an indication.             |
| “This signal shows the patient needs filling.” | “This finding suggests fluid responsiveness.” Only when that is the original, qualified interpretation.               |
| “The pressure is fixed.”                       | “Blood pressure has increased.” Do not add that shock or hypoperfusion has resolved.                                  |
| “Squeeze the circulation.”                     | “Titrate the vasopressor.” Only if titration is already the stated action; preserve agent, dose, target, and urgency. |

### E2 review triggers

Changing an estimated flow to a directly measured value; introducing a fluid-responsiveness threshold; changing timing of fluids or vasopressors; adding a universal mean arterial pressure goal; relabeling a filling pressure as volume; or transferring a waveform interpretation across a different ventilation/rhythm context. Preserve existing clinical validation requirements.

## Acid-base interpretation

**Source-anchored vocabulary:** acidemia/alkalemia; metabolic/respiratory acidosis and alkalosis; compensation; mixed acid-base disorder; bicarbonate; anion gap; strong ion difference; base excess; arterial/venous blood gas; PaCO2; clinical context.

Some raw forms are badly transcribed (for example “academic” in the pH discussion). Normalized spelling here is editorial, not a claim those exact spellings occur in the source.

Anchors: CC-02, 14:16–15:07, 2919–2951; 32:14–34:21, 3623–3707; 34:50–35:26, 3723–3747; 36:11–38:03, 3775–3847; 49:38–50:23, 4287–4319; 54:47–1:02:05, 4487–4775; 1:23:37–1:31:53, 5611–5935.

### Keep state and process distinct

The source explicitly distinguishes the pH state from underlying processes and discusses more than one process despite an apparently normal pH. This is a case where the word **state** is helpful and should not be removed because it sounded abstract in another module.

Use the conventional term alongside the explanation. For example, when the case already establishes it, “acidemia” names the pH finding while “metabolic acidosis” names a process. Do not infer the process from a low pH alone during copy editing.

### Preserve the speaker's ownership and limitations

“State, process, story” is described as a personal framework, not a published universal standard. It can remain an attributed teaching approach where selected, but must not replace established calculations or the entire site's acid-base method without explicit authorization. CC-02, 34:50–35:26, 3723–3747.

The session initially uses broad statements about chloride and later clarifies the relative sodium/chloride context. Read the later passage before extracting a rule. Do not implement a new strong-ion calculator, replace a full formula with a shortcut, or silently resolve disagreement between formulations.

### Preserve uncertainty in blood-gas interpretation

A later exchange emphasizes that a pattern should prompt closer evaluation rather than a definitive diagnosis in isolation. Use that reasoning style: explain what a finding suggests and what remains to be assessed. Do not turn a proposed chronic baseline into a measured baseline or state that an acute problem is absent when it has not been excluded. CC-02, 49:38–50:23, 4287–4319.

### E2 review triggers

Fixed VBG-to-ABG conversions; introducing or removing compensation formulas; substituting a bedside PaCO2 heuristic for a validated equation; numerical treatment thresholds; new bicarbonate indications; labeling compensation as an independent primary disorder; or making an absolute chloride value a complete diagnosis. The language pass may identify these issues but must not resolve them by model memory.

### Illustrative safe and unsafe edits

- Safe when the original diagnosis is explicit: “an added respiratory acid process” → “a concurrent respiratory acidosis.”
- Not safe without more context: “the pH is low” → “the patient has metabolic acidosis.”
- Safe style: “Interpret these numbers with the patient story” → “Interpret the blood gas in the patient's clinical context.”
- Not safe: a normal reference-range PaCO2 described as “adequate ventilation” without the case's clinical/acid-base context.

Keep helpful conceptual teaching, but do not convert an analogy about vectors or buffering into a literal new simulation equation.
