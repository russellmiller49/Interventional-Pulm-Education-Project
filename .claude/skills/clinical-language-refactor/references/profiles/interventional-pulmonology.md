# Interventional pulmonology language profile

## Scope and source status

Starting language references: IP-01 peripheral bronchoscopy; IP-02 EBUS/staging; IP-03 rigid-bronchoscopy anesthesia; IP-04 nonmalignant pleural effusions/IPC; IP-05 nonmalignant central airway obstruction. See `references/corpus-map.md` for exact files and episode ranges.

The terms below are normalized editorial vocabulary anchored to these sessions. They are not verbatim quotations, a current guideline summary, or authorization to add the surrounding speaker recommendations. Source dates, missing visuals, transcription quality, and individual practice preferences remain relevant.

## Peripheral bronchoscopy and airway anatomy

**Use:** bronchoscopy; bronchoscopist; live endoscopic image; virtual navigation image; CT-anatomic correlation; bronchial branch; segmental/subsegmental airway; airway–lesion relationship; radial EBUS; concentric/eccentric view; guide sheath; lesion localization; biopsy tool; nondiagnostic specimen; rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE).

Anchors: IP-01, 3:58–4:10, source lines 153–161; 8:05–9:34, 317–373; 17:05–18:56, 669–741; 22:27–24:00, 877–933; 36:32–39:01, 1417–1513.

Name the structure instead of calling it a path node, destination, tissue bucket, or sampling object. Use a real airway name or station only when the content identifies it. Do not infer a branch from an unavailable video or a screen-coordinate instruction.

The merged peripheral-imaging pass (PR #169) settled the fluoroscopy and navigation vocabulary — C-arm projection, collimation, CT-to-body divergence, navigation target, tool-in-lesion, concentric/eccentric radial EBUS views. Read `references/approved-examples.md` before rewording those topics in another module.

Preserve distinctions between reaching the virtual target, identifying a lesional rEBUS pattern, positioning a sampling tool, and obtaining diagnostic tissue. Do not convert a tool or catheter location into successful tissue acquisition.

The speaker discusses proximal and distal lesion borders and a target zone. Keep this as a source-specific teaching concept when useful; do not turn an illustrative insertion distance into a universal safety margin or every needle into the same sampling geometry. IP-01, 22:27–24:00, 877–933.

The Q&A clarifies that a demonstration of withdrawing and reintroducing the bronchoscope is not the speaker's routine workflow. Consult IP-01, 50:00–51:01, 1929–1965 before writing tool-exchange instructions from the earlier video narration.

**Editorial example — not an actual repository quote:**

- Before: “The map says the hardware reached the neighborhood.”
- After: “The catheter has reached the navigation target.”
- Preserve any separate statement about whether the lesion or sampling-tool position is confirmed. Do not add confirmation that was not present.

## Radial versus convex-probe EBUS

Use the modality name. Do not use “ultrasound” so generally that a reader cannot distinguish radial EBUS from convex-probe EBUS or another ultrasound examination.

“Concentric” and “eccentric” belong in the primary terminology, with a brief description of the image if needed. Do not replace a no-lesion finding with “no tissue,” which could imply something not actually observed.

An eccentric radial image does not by itself establish patient-coordinate direction. The supplied peripheral talk uses CT-anatomic correlation to supply directional interpretation: IP-01, 31:30–32:11, 1221–1249. Preserve that distinction; do not manufacture an anterior/posterior or clock-face rule.

## EBUS-TBNA and mediastinal staging

**Use:** EBUS-TBNA; lymph-node station; hilar/mediastinal lymph node; systematic nodal assessment; systematic versus targeted sampling; EUS-B when the actual transesophageal EBUS-scope approach is meant; needle gauge; stylet; suction; echogenicity; margins; central hilar structure; necrosis; elastography; tissue stiffness.

Anchors: IP-02, 4:26–4:51, 2487–2503; 18:02–18:57, 2975–3007; 21:02–21:51, 3083–3115; 43:45–45:27, 3911–3971; 46:01–47:03, 3991–4027; 56:38, 4387.

Separate **inspection order** from **needle-sampling order** when either is described. Do not collapse both into “work through the stations” if that erases what is happening at each stage. The transcript explicitly differentiates the two in its example; the language pass is not permission to change the protocol.

Suspicious ultrasound morphology, elastographic stiffness, a positive cytology result, and a confirmed pathologic nodal stage are not interchangeable. Retain the test and certainty actually stated.

Device discussion contains personal preferences for needles, balloon use, and suction. Do not turn a preferred needle gauge into a mandatory setting. Do not infer “larger gauge” means larger physical diameter without checking the original intended meaning.

IP-02 explicitly discusses staging in 2023. Preserve or flag historical edition references rather than silently presenting that discussion as current staging policy.

**Illustrative rewrite:**

- Before: “Sort the tissue targets into their buckets before collection.”
- After: “Identify the lymph-node stations before sampling.”
- Only when that is already the task; do not add an examination or sampling sequence.

## Rigid bronchoscopy, anesthesia, and the shared airway

**Use:** rigid bronchoscope; flexible bronchoscope; central airway obstruction; tracheal/bronchial stenosis; stridor; orthopnea; airway patency; ventilation; oxygenation; conventional ventilation; jet ventilation; spontaneous ventilation; airway leak; capnography; aspiration risk; stent; fistula; airway-management plan.

Anchors: IP-03, 5:11–7:13, 4733–4821; 7:22–8:19, 4829–4871; 11:49–13:08, 5033–5093; 16:06–17:02, 5225–5267; 18:31–19:42, 5333–5387; 22:32–23:32, 5513–5555.

Keep airway access, ventilation, oxygenation, and hemodynamic stability distinct. Do not allow “the airway is secure” to replace a more limited observation such as a view of the glottis, tube placement, adequate chest movement, or an oxygen saturation.

Distinguish upper-airway access difficulty from intrathoracic central-airway obstruction when the case makes that distinction. Preserve case-specific dependence on anatomy, position, equipment, and multidisciplinary planning.

Jet-ventilation pressure, conventional ventilator pressures, FiO2, and measured oxygenation are not interchangeable controls/readouts. The module's real device bindings and approved sources determine the labels.

**Safety hold:** do not import anesthesia drug choices, induction sequence, laser/oxygen safety claims, airway-fire thresholds, rescue techniques, or equipment settings from conversational shorthand. See the specific IP-03 quarantine entry in the safeguards file. A polished instruction about these can materially change patient risk even if it looks like a language edit.

**Illustrative rewrite:**

- Before: “Make sure the access-and-breathing story is aligned.”
- After: “Agree on the airway-management and ventilation plan with anesthesia.”
- Only when the original instruction already requires that discussion.

## Pleural disease and indwelling pleural catheters

**Use:** pleural effusion; nonmalignant pleural effusion; indwelling pleural catheter (IPC); tunneled pleural catheter; thoracentesis; recurrent/refractory effusion; pleural drainage; symptom relief; pleurodesis; hepatic hydrothorax; complications; patient-centered outcomes.

Anchors: IP-04, 2:17–2:25, 7553–7559; 3:24–3:58, 7601–7623; 4:38–6:21, 7649–7733; 8:53–9:09, 7847–7859; 13:14, 8039.

The speaker explicitly prefers “nonmalignant” to “benign” effusion in this context. Use this as a contextual editorial preference, not permission to rename every benign diagnosis or quoted study category. IP-04, 5:16–5:56, 7685–7715.

Keep the **reason for drainage** and the **reported endpoint** explicit: symptom improvement, drainage frequency, pleurodesis, complications, and survival are not synonymous. Do not replace “the catheter drained fluid” with “the treatment was successful” when success was not defined.

Do not rewrite catheter removal, reduced output, or absent drainage as pleurodesis without the module's stated criteria. Do not add drainage volumes, frequency, thresholds, anticoagulation management, or indications from the transcript.

**Illustrative rewrite:**

- Before: “The drainage tool improves the patient's experience.”
- After: “Pleural drainage improved the patient's reported dyspnea.”
- Only when dyspnea improvement is actually documented. Otherwise preserve the narrower observation.

## Nonmalignant central airway obstruction and stenosis

**Use:** central airway obstruction; laryngotracheal stenosis; postintubation/post-tracheostomy stenosis; idiopathic stenosis; intrinsic obstruction; extrinsic compression; dynamic airway narrowing; stricture; lesion length; morphology; cartilage involvement; dilation; recurrence/restenosis.

Anchors: IP-05, 3:59–5:15, 10409–10463; 8:18–8:27, 10601–10607; 14:31–15:13, 10877–10907; 23:22–25:04, 11273–11351.

The talk itself emphasizes shared terminology and explicit classifications. Preserve which classification is being used rather than merging schemes. Do not equate narrowing by diameter with narrowing by cross-sectional area, or treat length alone as the definition of complexity if the stated system includes other features.

Do not replace “dynamic narrowing” with a particular malacia diagnosis unless the module establishes it. Do not collapse location, mechanism, etiology, and severity into one vague “blockage” label.

**Illustrative rewrite:**

- Before: “Describe how bad the narrowing is.”
- After: “Describe the location, extent, and stated severity of the airway stenosis.”
- Only include dimensions already requested by the module; do not impose new thresholds.

## IP title policy

Recognizable chapter titles are useful: “Systematic EBUS assessment,” “CT-anatomic correlation,” or “Pleural drainage outcomes.” But a case testing recognition of a complication should not be titled with that complication before the answer.

For example, “The catheter has stopped draining” can be an appropriate pre-answer presentation title; “Catheter obstruction” may prematurely diagnose the problem. The final heading depends on what the learner is being asked to infer.
