1. The clinical model that should organize the module

The core clinical decision should be represented as:

Stent plan=indication+mechanical job+anatomic fit+time horizon+tissue interface+surveillance/exit strategy

Physics should answer focused questions within that model:

What load is the stent being asked to oppose?
How does this architecture transmit that load?
How might it deform in a curve, bifurcation, eccentric stenosis, cough, or breathing cycle?
Where will it contact the airway?
What might move relative to the mucosa?
Where could secretions collect?
What happens when the anatomy or tumor changes?
How difficult will removal become with time?

It should not answer:

Which device is universally best?
Which stent has a “safe” radial force?
What tissue pressure this patient will experience.
What probability of granulation this patient has.
What exact amount of oversizing should be used without clinical context.
Whether a schematic deformation proves clinical superiority.

That boundary is consistent with the malignant-airway-stenting guideline, which permits either silicone or hybrid metallic stents and emphasizes patient factors, airway factors, local availability, and operator expertise rather than a universal material hierarchy (Chaddha et al., 2024). Bench measurements should remain tied to the tested device, fixture, direction, and endpoint rather than being converted into clinical thresholds (Jung et al., 2021; Ratnovsky et al., 2015).

2. Proposed six-lesson curriculum
   Lesson 1 — Should this airway be stented?

Clinical question: Is there a residual structural problem that requires a stent?

Opening paired cases

Purely intrinsic malignant obstruction that is successfully debulked, with a stable airway afterward.
Mixed obstruction with substantial residual extrinsic compression after the intraluminal component is treated.

Required learner decisions

Classify the obstruction as intrinsic, extrinsic, mixed, or dynamic.
Identify whether a residual mechanical problem remains.
Choose:
No stent now.
Stent is reasonable.
More information or another intervention is needed first.
State the anticipated patient-centered benefit.
Confirm that the downstream lung is potentially functional and that the proposed intervention is congruent with the overall treatment plan.

Optional physics lens

A simple visual comparing:

Material occupying the lumen.
External compression of the wall.
Dynamic deformation.
A stable airway after removal of intraluminal disease.

Do not expose force controls or architecture selection yet. The purpose is to clarify what failure mode remains, not to select a product.

Learner output

“A stent is/is not being considered because **_ remains after _**, and the intended clinical benefit is \_\_\_.”

The malignant WABIP guideline similarly organizes indication around symptomatic extrinsic or mixed obstruction, recurrent intrinsic obstruction, or significant residual stenosis after debulking—not around the material label of the stent (Chaddha et al., 2024).

Lesson 2 — Define the mechanical and clinical job

Clinical question: What exactly must the device accomplish?

Use four short selectable vignettes:

Persistent extrinsic left-mainstem compression.
Main-carinal disease.
Aerodigestive fistula.
Selected dynamic central-airway collapse being evaluated with a temporary trial.

Required learner decisions

Identify one or more jobs:

Oppose persistent external compression.
Stabilize an airway after treatment of intrinsic disease.
Maintain a dynamic segment during a defined trial.
Seal a fistula.
Anchor at a bifurcation.
Preserve selected distal branches.
Temporize while another therapy takes effect.

Then define constraints:

Target airway and distal airways that must remain patent.
Curvature, taper, and branch relationships.
Landing zones.
Expected treatment duration.
Whether later removal is important.
Whether anatomy or disease burden is likely to change.

Optional physics lens

Show only the mechanical phenomenon relevant to the chosen case:

Extrinsic load.
Curved centerline.
Repeated collapse.
Bifurcation load sharing.
Need for a continuous covered surface.

Learner output

A structured “job statement,” for example:

Maintain left-mainstem patency against persistent external compression across a curved segment while preserving both left-lobar pathways and retaining a feasible removal strategy.

This promotes the strongest existing content in the module: “a device name is not a treatment goal,” fit should precede force comparisons, future removal is a present-tense constraint, and surveillance belongs to the initial decision.

Lesson 3 — Choose an architecture, not merely a material

Clinical question: Which architectural family best matches the defined job and constraints?

Organize comparison around clinical characteristics rather than a catalog of physics terms:

Decision axis Questions for the learner
Removability Is planned removal likely? What happens if tissue incorporates into exposed cells or ends?
Coverage Is sealing or protection from ingrowth needed? What secretion and surface consequences accompany coverage?
Deployment Is rigid deployment acceptable? Is repositioning or customization likely to matter?
Geometry Is the segment straight, curved, tapered, bifurcated, or highly dynamic?
Functional lumen How much wall thickness occupies the available lumen?
Anchoring Does fixation come from studs, flares, directional geometry, a carinal saddle, tissue incorporation, or imposed contact?
Branch preservation Does the architecture threaten an adjacent orifice?
Time horizon How will the interface change over weeks or months?

Architecture families

Preserve the existing seven models, but present them as educational architecture families, not seven equally appropriate clinical options:

Studded molded silicone.
Directional or D-shaped silicone.
Silicone Y.
Free-crossing braid.
Covered captured braid.
Covered laser-cut lattice.
Partially covered single-wire knit.

The existing registry is particularly valuable here. It already distinguishes, for example, continuous-wall silicone from covered wire scaffolds, directional anchoring from whole-body compression, carinal fixation from straight-tube fixation, and partially covered midsections from exposed ends.

Required learner decision

For each case:

Choose a leading architecture family.
Choose a reasonable alternative.
Name one advantage.
Name two liabilities.
Explain what would change the decision.

Optional physics lens

A “Why does it behave this way?” drawer showing:

Solid wall versus wire scaffold.
Sliding crossings versus captured crossings.
Rings and connectors versus braid.
Single-wire loops versus multiwire braid.
Continuous wall versus covered cells.
Bifurcated load sharing.

Raw COF, RRF, hysteresis, force curves, and GINA–Dumon values move into an Advanced Engineering Deep Dive. They should not be required to complete this lesson.

Lesson 4 — Fit, airway geometry, and immediate failure modes

Clinical question: How could an otherwise reasonable architecture fail because of fit or anatomy?

Required anatomy checklist

Diameter at the intended landing zones.
Length of disease and intended coverage.
Taper.
Curvature.
Branch angles and branch orifices.
Proximal and distal landing-zone quality.
Dynamic change during breathing and cough.
Expected future tumor response or airway remodeling.
Relationship to a fistula or adjacent esophageal device.

Clinical presets for the 3D viewer

Replace the required-path “applied displacement” controls with authored case presets:

Undersized in a relatively straight segment
Inspect incomplete contact and migration potential.
Mismatch in a curved segment
Inspect inner-curve gapping, outer-curve contact, straightening tendency, and device ends.
Eccentric external compression
Inspect ovalization and asymmetric contact.
Bifurcation mismatch
Inspect limb angle, limb length, carinal saddle, and distal branch patency.
Repeated cough or respiratory motion
Inspect relative motion, cyclic bending, and repeated end contact.
Covered surface
Inspect where secretions might travel or collect.

These should be labeled as things to inspect, not calculated high-, medium-, or low-risk predictions. The current viewport already supports bending, ovalization, breathing, cough, deployment, and architecture-specific behavior, so this is primarily a control and copy refactor rather than a graphics rewrite.

Learner output

“The fit plan must account for **_, and the two failure modes I would specifically anticipate are _** and \_\_\_.”

Important content-control decision

The existing board chapter contains useful clinical topics but also exact diameter ranges, margin rules, complication percentages, and follow-up statements. Those values should not be copied automatically into the interactive module. Transfer them only after physician review and source-specific evidence attachment.

Lesson 5 — Complications, granulation, and surveillance

Clinical question: When the airway worsens after stenting, what failed—and why?

Organize the lesson around recurrent symptoms or obstruction rather than around a list of device complications.

Differential categories
Mucus plugging.
Granulation tissue.
Tumor ingrowth or overgrowth.
Migration.
Malposition or branch obstruction.
Recurrent external compression.
Infection or biofilm-associated obstruction.
Stent fracture.
Cover tear or delamination.
Fistula progression or failure to seal.
Required learner task

For each case, identify:

Most likely cause.
Dangerous alternatives.
Mechanistic contributors.
Findings needed to distinguish them.
Immediate restoration-of-patency priorities.
The underlying driver that must be corrected.
Whether the ongoing indication for the stent remains.
Follow-up and exit strategy.
Surveillance

Teach surveillance as part of the initial stent prescription rather than a postscript. For malignant CAO, the WABIP guideline suggests surveillance bronchoscopy even in asymptomatic patients and, in the absence of stronger evidence, suggests an initial examination at approximately 4–6 weeks. This is a conditional, context-specific recommendation rather than a universal schedule for every benign and malignant case (Chaddha et al., 2024).

Learner output

A four-column plan:

Expected failure mode Recognition trigger Response category Correct the underlying driver
Example: proximal granulation New cough, wheeze, secretion change, CT or bronchoscopic narrowing Restore patency and evaluate infection Reassess end position, fit, motion, ongoing indication, and need for exchange/removal
Lesson 6 — Integrated clinical assessment

Retain six cases initially, but rewrite them to test decisions rather than mechanics vocabulary:

No-stent case: Pure intrinsic malignant obstruction after successful debulking with stable residual wall.
Persistent extrinsic compression: Curved mainstem airway requiring an architecture and fit tradeoff.
Benign complex stenosis: Emphasis on definitive therapy, temporary support, removability, and exit plan.
Main-carinal disease: Whole-Y fit, limb lengths, branch angles, and distal patency.
Proximal granulation: Mechanical and infectious contributors, not ablation alone.
Post-treatment migration or mucus obstruction: Reassess changing anatomy, secretion burden, and ongoing indication.

Each case should test four domains:

Indication.
Architecture and fit.
Predicted complication.
Surveillance or exit plan.

Keep one-best-answer questions for the first refactor, but structure the data model so a later version can support multi-step branching.

3. The granulation-tissue experience

Granulation should become the module’s best example of physics informing clinical reasoning without overreaching.

The causal model to teach
Airway/device fit + focal end or edge contact + straightening or repeated relative motion + exposed cells, cover transitions, or surface properties + retained secretions, colonization, or infection + dwell time and host biology
↓
mucosal injury and inflammatory signaling
↓
wound-healing and foreign-body response
↓
granulation tissue that may re-obstruct the airway

The interface should explicitly label this as a plausible multifactorial pathway, not a validated patient-specific risk equation.

Granulation is a wound-healing and foreign-body response whose exact drivers remain incompletely understood. Mechanical injury, fit, infection, biofilm, surface properties, and host response may interact. Observational data associate lower-respiratory infection with subsequent granulation, and a study of Dumon stents found an association between stent-to-airway diameter relationship and granulation, but neither supports a universal formula or cutoff for all devices and patients (Gupta et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2011; Ost et al., 2012).

Recommended case

Vignette

A patient initially improves after stenting of a curved main bronchus. Several weeks later he develops recurrent cough, increasing secretions, and focal narrowing near the proximal end. Purulent material is present around the stent.

Interaction 1: Recognize the pattern

Choose among:

Mucus plugging only.
Granulation at the device end.
Tumor recurrence.
Migration.
Structural stent failure.
More than one process may coexist.

Interaction 2: Identify contributors

The learner should consider:

End position relative to the curve.
Stiffness transition at the end.
Repeated contact during breathing or cough.
Poorly matched landing zone.
Secretions and infection.
Coverage or exposed-cell transitions.
Continuing need for the same stent.
Host and disease factors.

Interaction 3: Open the physics lens

Show the stent in a curved airway and highlight:

Inner-curve gapping.
Outer-curve contact.
End displacement.
Repeated motion.

The caption should say:

This scene shows a possible location of repeated contact. It does not calculate tissue pressure or prove the cause of granulation in an individual patient.

Interaction 4: Construct the clinical response

Require four categories rather than one procedural answer:

Restore airway patency.
Evaluate and manage secretion or infectious burden.
Reassess fit, position, architecture, and ongoing indication.
Decide whether repositioning, exchange, removal, or another strategy is required.

This avoids the common educational error of teaching repeated ablation as the complete solution while leaving the mechanical or infectious driver unchanged.

11. Definition of done

The salvage is successful when:

The first screen presents a patient, not a force diagram.
Every required lesson starts with a clinical decision.
Physics is available at the moment it can explain or change that decision.
A learner can finish the module without opening the engineering deep dive.
A learner who opens the physics lens receives additional explanatory value.
At least one correct answer is “do not place a stent.”
Architecture is taught separately from material and brand.
Fit includes curvature, taper, landing zones, branches, and changing anatomy.
Granulation is taught as a multifactorial tissue response.
Complication management includes correction of drivers, not merely treatment of the obstructing tissue.
Every stent choice includes a surveillance and exit strategy.
No simulation produces clinical superiority, force thresholds, tissue pressure, or individual risk.
Existing 3D development effort is preserved.
Old links and saved progress degrade gracefully.
Clinical content remains visibly marked as draft until physician-reviewed.
