Lung Cancer Staging and Linear EBUS

Exam Mapping & Scope

This chapter integrates the 9th edition TNM stage classification with practical, exam‑relevant application of linear endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS) for mediastinal staging. It emphasizes imaging triage (CT, PET/CT, brain MRI when appropriate), nodal maps and boundaries, indications for invasive staging, systematic vs. targeted EBUS strategies, sample acquisition/processing, complication recognition, and post‑test decision‑making (including when—if ever—to confirm a negative EBUS with mediastinoscopy).

Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, the reader will be able to:

Apply 9th edition TNM updates—specifically N2a vs. N2b and M1c1 vs. M1c2—to clinical staging decisions.
Identify which mediastinal/hilar nodal stations are accessible by EBUS vs. EUS‑B and recognize high‑risk anatomic boundaries.
Select patients for invasive mediastinal staging and choose between systematic and targeted EBUS strategies.
Execute a stepwise, contamination‑minimizing EBUS‑TBNA workflow, including proper sampling order (N3 → N2 → N1) and ensuring adequate passes.
Optimize sedation/oxygenation and troubleshoot poor acoustic windows, calcified or necrotic nodes, and bleeding.
Interpret negative invasive staging in context and plan when additional mediastinoscopy changes management.

High‑Yield One‑Pager (Pearls, Pitfalls, Traps)

TNM‑9 Essentials: N2 subdivides into single‑station (N2a) vs. multistation (N2b); M1c subdivides into single‑organ (M1c1) vs. multi‑organ (M1c2). Several T/N combinations shift stage groups (notably T1N2aM0 → Stage IIB).
Sizing Rules: Measure the solid/invasive component for T size; lepidic/ground‑glass portions do not count toward T size; STAS does not change T category.
Node Map Boundaries Matter:
The lower rim of the azygos vein distinguishes 4R from 10R.
The superior rim of the aortic arch separates 2L from 4L.
Pearl: Mislabeling these boundaries can artificially upstage or downstage a patient.
Imaging Triage: PET/CT outperforms CT for mediastinal nodes but has false positives. Do not rely on PET/CT for small brain mets—use brain MRI when clinically indicated.
Who Needs Invasive Staging?
Discrete mediastinal adenopathy or PET‑avid mediastinal nodes.
“Intermediate risk” scenarios: Normal CT/PET mediastinum but a central tumor, tumor &gt;3 cm, or cN1.
EBUS vs. Surgery: Endoscopic needle staging (EBUS/EUS‑B) has high sensitivity/specificity and is the preferred first test. Combining EBUS + EUS increases yield.
Systematic &gt; Targeted: A systematic approach (sampling ≥3 stations, typically 4R, 4L, 7) detects additional N2/N3 disease compared with “hit‑and‑run” targeted strategies.
Sampling Order (Exam Favorite): Always sample from N3 → N2 → N1 to avoid contamination.
Adequacy: ≥3 passes per target is a common benchmark; consider additional passes for molecular testing or when ROSE is unavailable.
Stations 5–6 Caveat: Rarely accessible by EBUS; consider EUS‑B or surgical approaches when these are critical.
Complications: Major events are rare. Pneumothorax is unusual (&lt;1%) but can occur near the pleura (e.g., right paratracheal station close to pleural reflection).
Negative EBUS—Then What? Confirmatory mediastinoscopy after a high‑quality, systematic negative EBUS yields limited incremental benefit in many settings; reserve for select high-risk patients or institutional protocols.
Sedation: Moderate and deep sedation are both acceptable. Deep sedation may reduce intraprocedural instability; HFNC can mitigate desaturation under moderate sedation.
Special Situations: EBUS‑TBNA has limited utility for classic Hodgkin lymphoma; cryobiopsy or surgical tissue is often needed for architecture.
Report Like a Surgeon: Specify stations sampled, needle gauge, passes, use of ROSE, and any limitations (calcification, necrosis, vascular proximity).

Core Concepts

Pathophysiology / Epidemiology (Brief)

Nodal spread follows predictable drainage from lobar to mediastinal stations. Prognosis is tied not just to the presence of nodal disease but also to its distribution (single vs. multistation) and extent (ipsilateral vs. contralateral). TNM‑9 formalizes this clinically observed gradient by splitting N2 and M1c.

Indications & Contraindications

Indications for invasive mediastinal staging (needle-based preferred first test):

Discrete mediastinal lymph node enlargement (short axis &gt;1 cm) and/or FDG‑avid mediastinal nodes with no distant metastases.
“Intermediate risk” with radiographically normal mediastinum but high‑risk primary: central tumor, tumor &gt;3 cm, or suspected N1 on imaging.
Select PET‑negative primaries (low SUV) where occult N2/N3 risk remains.

When invasive staging is generally NOT indicated:

Peripheral tumor &lt;3 cm with avid primary and no suspicious nodes on CT/PET (proceed to resection).

EBUS/EUS‑B Station Selection:

EBUS: Reaches most stations except 5–6 (aortopulmonary and para‑aortic).
EUS‑B: Adds excellent access to stations 8–9 and augments access to 4L and 7; the left adrenal gland is often assessable during the same session.

Contraindications and Precautions:

Uncontrolled coagulopathy or high bleeding risk (pause therapeutic anticoagulation when safe).
For EUS‑B, esophageal varices or severe dysphagia increase risk.

Pre‑procedure Evaluation

Imaging: Chest CT (thin sections); PET/CT to screen for distant disease and prioritize stations; brain MRI case‑by‑case (e.g., neurologic symptoms or curative intent).
Labs and Meds: Review platelet count/coagulation and antithrombotics with a plan for holding/resumption per local protocol.
Airway & Anesthesia Plan: Moderate sedation vs. deep sedation/GA. GA facilitates stability, suppresses cough, and allows repeated scope exchanges (ETT ≥8.0, LMA, or rigid support). For moderate sedation, HFNC reduces desaturation versus low‑flow oxygen.
Consent: Document potential benefits (minimally invasive staging, molecular adequacy) and rare but serious risks (significant bleeding, pneumothorax, mediastinitis).

Equipment & Setup

Scope: Linear (curvilinear) echo‑bronchoscope with ~7.5‑MHz transducer; outer diameter ≈6.3–6.6 mm. Balloon sheath for coupling (inflate with saline).
Needles: 19G, 21G, 22G, 25G; typical excursion depth ~5–40 mm with adjustable safety lock.
Ancillaries: Doppler, suction, syringes for vacuum (or capillary technique), ROSE (if available), media for cell block/core, HFNC or ventilatory support.
Layout: Sterile field, bite block/mouthpiece, crash‑cart readiness. Position the ultrasound machine so the operator can simultaneously see endoscopic and US views.

Step‑by‑Step Technique / Procedural Checklist

Access: Airway access (mouth/LMA/ETT/rigid) and topicalization; couple balloon for ultrasound contact.
Survey: Align on CT/PET roadmap; confirm vascular landmarks on US (e.g., SVC and azygos for 4R; left PA and aortic arch for 4L).
Systematic Sampling Order: Contralateral mediastinal (N3) → Ipsilateral mediastinal (N2) → Hilar (N1).
Targeting & Safety: Activate Doppler to avoid vessels; respect pleura near right paratracheal/azygos region; differentiate esophagus posteriorly at station 7.
Needle Handling (Jab Method): Lock body, advance sheath to visual field, release lock, quick jab into node under real‑time US, stylet manipulation, apply suction, 3–10 to‑and‑fro motions, release suction, retract, and recover sample.
Passes: Perform ≥3 passes per station for cytology; add more if molecular testing is anticipated or ROSE is inadequate.
Processing: Prepare smears + cell block (or clot core) for histology and ancillary studies; label by station.
EUS-B: Combine with EUS‑B (same session) if posterior/inferior stations or left adrenal are indicated.
Completion: Rinse scope, reinflate balloon as needed to confirm hemostasis, and perform brief airway inspection.

Troubleshooting & Intra‑procedure Management

Poor Contact: Refill balloon, adjust flexion, change patient neck position, or use small scope tip deflections to optimize the window.
Calcified or Fibrotic Nodes: Use a stiffer needle (e.g., 19–21G) and multiple redirections within the node; consider additional passes for cell block.
Necrotic Nodes: Sample the viable peripheral rim on US; use Doppler to avoid feeding vessels. Note: Necrotic centers are typically hypoechoic or anechoic.
Bleeding: Most events are minor; apply suction, cold saline; if persistent, withdraw and reassess coagulation status; consider tamponade with the scope/balloon.
Cough/Movement: Deepen sedation or convert to GA; LMA/ETT often improves precision and throughput.
Station Errors: Re‑interrogate landmarks (azygos arch = inferior 4R border; superior LPA = inferior 4L border; esophagus posterior at 7). Misclassification can change N category.

Post‑procedure Care & Follow‑up

Monitor 1–2 hours for hypoxemia or bleeding.
Resume antithrombotics per protocol; provide return precautions (fever, chest pain, hemoptysis).
Reporting Essentials: Clinical context, imaging summary, stations evaluated and sampled, gauge and passes, ROSE result (if used), immediate complications/limitations, and next steps.

Complications (Prevention, Recognition, Management)

Overall Risk: Low. Major adverse events are rare (&lt;2%).
Pneumothorax: Rare (&lt;1%); risk increases when pleura is close (e.g., high right paratracheal). Always keep pleura in view while sampling.
Infectious: Mediastinitis is rare. Sterile technique reduces risk. Consider antibiotic prophylaxis selectively (e.g., cystic lesions, high‑risk hosts).
Sedation‑related: Hypoventilation, hypotension, arrhythmia. Optimize preoxygenation (HFNC); deep sedation/GA can reduce intra‑procedure instability in some cohorts.

Special Populations

cN0 “Intermediate Risk”: Central tumors, &gt;3 cm, or cN1—systematic EBUS/EUS‑B is appropriate; occult N2/N3 disease is not rare.
LUL Tumors (Aortopulmonary Window): Consider EUS‑B or surgical access to stations 5–6 if critical for management.
Lymphoma: Classic Hodgkin lymphoma is challenging by TBNA; consider adjunctive cryobiopsy or alternate approach for architecture.
Radiation Planning: Pre‑treatment EBUS/EUS‑B improves target delineation when nodal status is uncertain.

Evidence & Outcomes (Selected)

Imaging Performance: PET/CT mediastinal nodal sensitivity/specificity is ~77–85% / 86–90%. CT alone is ~55–61% / 79–81%. PET/CT has false positives (inflammation) and false negatives (low‑FDG histologies).
Endoscopic Staging: EBUS‑TBNA sensitivity is ~90% with near‑perfect specificity. Combining EBUS+EUS raises sensitivity into the mid‑90% range.
Systematic vs. Targeted: Systematic staging (sampling ≥3 stations) detects additional N2/N3 disease compared with targeted “suspicious‑only” approaches.
After Negative EBUS: Routine confirmatory mediastinoscopy adds little for many patients; reserve for select high‑risk scenarios or discordant cases.

Algorithm 2 — Linear EBUS‑TBNA Procedural Workflow

Pre-op: Review imaging, coagulation, sedation.
Setup: Airway access, balloon coupling, ultrasound check.
Map: Identify vascular landmarks and station boundaries.
Sample: N3 → N2 → N1 (Jab technique, suction).
Adequacy: ≥3 passes/station (more for molecular). ROSE review if available.
Adjunct: Add EUS-B for 7/8/9/Left Adrenal if needed.
Finish: Hemostasis check, structured report.

Cases & Applied Learning

Case 1 (Next best step) A 5‑cm left lower lobe NSCLC is PET‑avid; mild uptake in 4L, 7, and 11R; no distant metastases. Answer: B. Start sampling at 11R. Start at N3 first (contralateral hilar 11R, if right-sided nodes were involved, but here 11R is contralateral to LLL), then N2 (4L/7), then N1. Note: Ensure station laterality in question matches the "N3 first" logic.

Case 2 (Access planning) A left upper lobe tumor with suspected aortopulmonary window involvement on PET/CT. Answer: B. EUS‑B or surgical approach to 5–6. Stations 5–6 are rarely accessible by EBUS.

Case 3 (Negative EBUS) A patient with central T2 tumor and cN0 by CT/PET undergoes systematic EBUS/EUS‑B (≥3 stations, adequate passes) that is negative. Answer: B. Proceed with resection/RT per plan. High‑quality negative endosonography often obviates routine confirmatory mediastinoscopy.

Controversies, Variability, and Evolving Evidence

Confirmatory Mediastinoscopy: Modern trials show small incremental detection (particularly single‑station N2) with trade-offs in delay/morbidity; practices vary by center.
"Zone" Definitions: TNM‑9’s N2a/N2b helps clarify prognostic separation, but clinical thresholds for resectability remain multidisciplinary.
Sedation Strategy: Choice between moderate and deep sedation depends on institutional expertise and patient comorbidities.
Cryobiopsy: EBUS‑guided mediastinal cryobiopsy is emerging for situations where TBNA is insufficient (e.g., lymphoma architecture, broad molecular profiling).

Take‑Home Checklist

Know TNM‑9: N2a vs. N2b; M1c1 vs. M1c2; T counts solid/invasive size only.
Triage: CT + PET/CT first; brain MRI selectively.
Invasive Staging: Indicated for radiographic mediastinal disease and “intermediate‑risk” primaries.
Technique: Systematic EBUS±EUS‑B; N3 → N2 → N1; ≥3 passes per station.
Landmarks: Azygos (4R/10R); Aortic Arch/LPA (4L).
Safety: Doppler for vessels; respect pleura at 4R.
EUS-B: Use for stations 7 (posterior), 8–9, and left adrenal.
Reporting: Structured report with stations, passes, adequacy, and limitations.
Negative EBUS: Does not always require mediastinoscopy—decide case‑by‑case.
