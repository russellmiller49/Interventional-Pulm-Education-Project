Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. This episode discusses Lin and colleagues, published in the Journal of Bronchology & Interventional Pulmonology in 2025, on video-assisted thoracoscopic versus percutaneous tunneled pleural catheter placement and pleurodesis outcomes. The question is highly practical: does the way a tunneled pleural catheter is inserted influence the chance that the catheter can later be removed because pleurodesis has occurred?
Guest: That is a meaningful endpoint for patients. TPCs provide symptom palliation, but catheter independence is often a major goal when life expectancy and functional status make it realistic. The catheter is helpful, but living with chronic drainage, supplies, caregiver dependence, and infection risk is still a burden.
Narrator: The investigators performed a retrospective single-center study of patients who had TPC placement between 2010 and 2018. TPCs were placed by thoracic surgery, interventional pulmonology, or interventional radiology. The final analysis included 312 patients. Pleurodesis success was defined as catheter removal within 90 days because of low or absent drainage, excluding removals for catheter malfunction, infection, patient preference for a different treatment, or other catheter-related complications.
Guest: The headline result was striking. Pleurodesis success occurred in 61.2% of thoracoscopically placed TPCs compared with 24.9% of percutaneously placed TPCs, with P < .001. In a Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for factors such as sclerosant use and pleural biopsies, thoracoscopic placement was still associated with about a twofold greater chance of pleurodesis, with a hazard ratio of 2.04.
Narrator: On first read, that seems to favor thoracoscopic placement strongly. But the central limitation is confounding by service line and drainage protocol. The paper notes that interventional pulmonology and thoracic surgery instructed patients to drain daily, whereas interventional radiology used a symptom-guided drainage protocol.
Guest: That is a major issue because randomized data from ASAP and AMPLE-2 support more frequent drainage as a way to increase spontaneous pleurodesis. So if one group gets daily drainage and another gets symptom-guided drainage, the observed difference may reflect catheter management as much as insertion technique.
Narrator: The service-level outcomes make that concern more visible. Pleurodesis success was only 7.6% in the IR group, 34.3% in the IP group, and 48.9% in the thoracic surgery group. Since IR placed catheters percutaneously and used symptom-guided drainage, the effect of technique and drainage frequency cannot be separated cleanly in that comparison.
Guest: The authors did try to address this with a subanalysis limited to the IP and thoracic surgery patients, both of whom used daily drainage. In that more comparable drainage-protocol subgroup, thoracoscopic placement still carried a 2.6-times greater chance of pleurodesis success. That is probably the most persuasive finding in the paper because it reduces, though does not eliminate, the drainage-protocol confounder.
Narrator: Other selection biases remain. This was a single-center retrospective study, and the patient populations differed by service. Thoracic surgery cared for a much higher proportion of patients with mesothelioma, around 36% of their cohort, compared with almost none in the IP and IR cohorts. Mesothelioma may have different pleural biology and different pleurodesis dynamics. Regression can adjust for measured differences, but it cannot fully erase retrospective selection.
Guest: Performance status is another unmeasured concern. The authors note that patients with poorer functional status may have been preferentially selected for the less invasive percutaneous approach. That would bias the percutaneous group toward lower pleurodesis success independent of technique. Frailer patients may drain less consistently, live a shorter time, have trapped lung, or be less likely to return for protocolized follow-up.
Narrator: The proposed mechanism for thoracoscopic benefit is plausible. Thoracoscopy allows more complete initial fluid evacuation, direct pleural inspection, possible adhesiolysis, pleural biopsy, and better pleural apposition from the outset. All of those could help create conditions for pleurodesis. Percutaneous placement, especially if the pleural space is not fully evacuated or the lung is not fully expandable, may start from a less favorable mechanical position.
Guest: But the tradeoff is substantial. Thoracoscopic placement usually requires more procedural infrastructure, often general anesthesia and an operating room or advanced thoracoscopy environment. Percutaneous TPC placement can often be performed with local anesthesia in a procedure suite, radiology suite, or even at the bedside. The best approach may depend heavily on patient goals, prognosis, performance status, lung expandability, and local resources.
Narrator: That is the practical clinical translation. For a patient with reasonable functional status, expected survival long enough for catheter independence to matter, and a strong goal of catheter removal, thoracoscopic placement plus daily drainage may be worth considering. For a frail patient with limited prognosis, high anesthesia risk, or a primary goal of rapid symptom relief, a percutaneous catheter may remain the more appropriate option.
Guest: The paper also reinforces that catheter insertion and catheter management should not be treated as separate worlds. A TPC program needs a drainage protocol, follow-up structure, criteria for removal, patient education, and multidisciplinary alignment. The device does not produce pleurodesis by itself; the entire pleural workflow matters.
Narrator: Let's play devil's advocate on the endpoint. Removal by 90 days is patient-centered in one sense, but it is also influenced by follow-up behavior, drainage instructions, service preferences, and thresholds for declaring pleurodesis. A prospective trial would need standardized drainage, standardized removal criteria, assessment of lung expansion, and probably stratification by malignant versus nonmalignant effusion and by tumor type.
Guest: Agreed. The study does not prove that thoracoscopic TPC placement should replace percutaneous placement broadly. It does suggest that insertion technique may be a modifiable contributor to pleurodesis success, particularly when paired with daily drainage and careful patient selection.
Narrator: The synthesis is that Lin and colleagues provide provocative retrospective evidence that thoracoscopic TPC placement is associated with higher pleurodesis success than percutaneous placement. The effect remains compelling in the daily-drainage subgroup, but residual confounding is substantial.
Guest: For IP clinicians, the practical message is to match the technique to the goal. If the goal is palliation with minimal procedural burden, percutaneous TPC placement remains highly valuable. If the goal is catheter removal and the patient is a suitable procedural candidate, thoracoscopic placement deserves consideration. What the field needs next is a prospective comparison with standardized drainage and removal criteria.
Narrator: That closes this episode of InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. Thanks for listening.
