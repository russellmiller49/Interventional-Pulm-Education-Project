Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we're reviewing Westley and colleagues, published in Respiration, on local anesthetic thoracoscopy practice in the United Kingdom in 2024. The paper is a snapshot survey of how LAT is being performed and how practice appears to have shifted since a similar survey in 2017.

Guest: This is useful because it gives the pleural community a mirror. The survey was distributed electronically through the UK Pleural Society, and the authors received responses from 37 UK centers. The core question was not whether LAT works, but how it is actually being delivered - patient selection, sedation, premedication, equipment, admission practices, and safety infrastructure.

Narrator: So let's start with the headline. Is LAT still the preferred next step for an undiagnosed exudative effusion after negative cytology?

Guest: Mostly, yes, but less uniformly than before. Eighty-seven percent of centers still preferred LAT as the first-line investigation after negative cytology, down from about 95% in the 2017 survey. A small number of centers - three - now preferred ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy as the initial invasive step.

Narrator: That is a meaningful signal, even though the sample is small. It suggests that ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy may be gaining ground in selected workflows, particularly where it is faster, less resource-intensive, or better suited to the local service model.

Guest: The bigger operational shift is post-procedure management. In 2017, 76% of centers routinely admitted patients after LAT. In the 2024 survey, that fell to 51%. That is a major movement toward ambulatory or day-case LAT.

Narrator: Which is a major workflow issue. LAT has historically been treated as a procedure that often required admission, chest tube management, and observation. If centers are moving toward day-case care, that affects staffing, recovery areas, pleural nurse involvement, post-procedure imaging, drainage strategy, and escalation planning.

Guest: The survey also emphasizes persistent variation. Premedication is a good example. Only about 30% of centers used any premedication beyond sedation, and the agents varied widely: tranexamic acid, metoclopramide, atropine, NSAIDs. Eighty-one percent did not routinely give prophylactic antibiotics, and routine IV fluid administration was uncommon at 22%.

Narrator: Sedation was more consistent. Ninety-five percent of centers routinely used sedation, and 87% used midazolam plus an opioid, typically administered by physicians or nurses. Only one center reported anesthetist-led propofol sedation.

Guest: That consistency probably reflects the way LAT is embedded in respiratory-led pleural services. But the variation in other domains matters, especially because LAT sits in a gray zone between endoscopy, minor surgery, and pleural intervention.

Narrator: Let's critically assess the study itself. This is a survey, so recall bias and sampling bias are baked in. The authors did not know the true denominator - how many UK centers are performing LAT - so representativeness is uncertain.

Guest: Exactly. Distribution through the UK Pleural Society likely enriches for engaged, higher-volume, or academically connected pleural centers. So if anything, this may be a best-case snapshot. The wider practice environment could be even more heterogeneous.

Narrator: The equipment data are also interesting. Semi-rigid thoracoscopy appears to have increased: exclusive use was 8% of centers in 2017 and 22% in this survey.

Guest: Rigid thoracoscopy still predominated, used by 73% of sites. Semi-rigid scopes may be attractive because many pulmonologists are already familiar with flexible endoscopic handling, and diagnostic yield is generally considered comparable in appropriate settings. But equipment choice is not just about diagnostic performance; it also affects biopsy size, procedural ergonomics, teaching, repair costs, and compatibility with local sterilization and staffing workflows.

Narrator: Here is the devil's advocate question: does all this variation actually matter? Major complications after LAT are generally uncommon, and variation may simply reflect local expertise and resources.

Guest: That is the key tension. Variation itself is not automatically bad. But the concerning finding is that 73% of centers did not have standard operating procedures for managing complications. And only 30% had on-site cardiothoracic surgical backup. In that setting, even a rare complication - an intercostal artery bleed, persistent air leak, severe hypoxemia, or failure of lung re-expansion - can become high stakes quickly.

Narrator: That may be the most important safety message in the paper. The issue is not whether every center should use the same scope or the same sedative combination. The issue is whether every center has a documented plan for predictable failure modes.

Guest: Exactly. LAT is a mature procedure, but maturity can create a false sense of security. A low event rate does not remove the need for rehearsed escalation pathways, particularly in hospitals without thoracic surgery immediately available.

Narrator: The authors also argue for a central database for reporting LAT procedures and complications, analogous to the National Endoscopy Database in the UK. Seventy-six percent of centers already maintained a local database, so scaling to a national dataset may be feasible.

Guest: That would be valuable. A central database could move the field beyond periodic surveys and toward longitudinal auditing of volume, diagnostic yield, bleeding, infection, pain, trapped lung, readmission, persistent air leak, and downstream interventions. It could also help define minimum standards for day-case LAT.

Narrator: What does this mean beyond the UK?

Guest: It provides a useful benchmark for any pleural service. The exact numbers may not translate to other health systems, but the themes do: LAT is moving toward more ambulatory care, ultrasound-guided pleural biopsy may be competing in some diagnostic pathways, and procedural standardization remains incomplete.

Narrator: The practical takeaway is that LAT programs should not confuse local habit with standard practice. The survey supports reviewing sedation protocols, premedication policies, post-procedure drainage strategy, discharge criteria, complication SOPs, and data capture.

Guest: And the emphasis should be minimum safety standards rather than rigid uniformity. A high-volume center with rigid thoracoscopy, inpatient drainage, and thoracic surgery backup may reasonably look different from a respiratory-led day-case semi-rigid program. But both should have explicit criteria for patient selection, escalation, and follow-up.
