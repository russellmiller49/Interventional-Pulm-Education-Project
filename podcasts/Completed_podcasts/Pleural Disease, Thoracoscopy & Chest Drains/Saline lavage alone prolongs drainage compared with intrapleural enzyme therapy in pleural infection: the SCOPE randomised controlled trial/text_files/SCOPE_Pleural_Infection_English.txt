InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review: Saline Lavage versus Intrapleural Enzyme Therapy for Pleural Infection

Estimated duration: 9 to 10 minutes

Article type: Original research, randomized controlled trial

Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we are looking at Porcel and colleagues, published in the European Respiratory Journal in 2026, on the SCOPE randomized controlled trial. The practical question is straightforward: in adults with complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema, does intrapleural saline lavage, either alone or added to intrapleural enzyme therapy, improve pleural drainage compared with enzyme therapy alone?

Guest: Right, and this is a useful question because saline lavage has always had a plausible mechanical appeal. Infected pleural spaces are full of fibrin, debris, viscous fluid, pus, and loculations. So it is reasonable to ask whether physically washing the space could improve drainage or make fibrinolytic therapy work better. But plausible is not the same as beneficial, and that is where this trial is helpful.

Narrator: SCOPE was a two-center, prospective, randomized superiority trial in Spain. Adults were eligible if the pleural fluid was consistent with complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema. That meant frank pus, pleural fluid pH less than 7.2, glucose less than 60 milligrams per deciliter, or a positive pleural fluid culture. The trial excluded patients with conditions such as pregnancy, allergy to urokinase or D-Nase, active bleeding, recent major surgery or trauma, recent stroke, tuberculous pleuritis, and pleural infection related to recent thoracic surgery or trauma.

Guest: The comparator is important. This was not saline lavage versus chest tube drainage alone. The standard-care arm was intrapleural enzyme therapy, which is much closer to contemporary pleural practice in many centers. Patients were randomized one to one to one into three groups: saline lavage alone, saline lavage plus I E T, or I E T alone. The I E T regimen was one hundred thousand units of urokinase plus 10 milligrams of D-Nase, given concurrently through the chest catheter, with a 1.5 hour dwell time. Treatments were repeated once daily for up to 6 days.

Narrator: Chest tube technique also matters. Small-bore tubes, 14 French or smaller, were used in more than 90 percent of cases overall, inserted with the Seldinger technique under ultrasound guidance. After tube placement, fluid was aspirated as completely as tolerated. For saline lavage, the protocol used manual instillation of 0.9 percent saline through the chest tube, followed by immediate aspiration until the fluid was clear. So this was active manual lavage, not passive irrigation and not a continuous lavage system.

Guest: The primary outcome was duration of pleural drainage, from randomization to chest tube removal. That is a procedure-centered endpoint, but it still matters. It affects pain, mobility, nursing workload, imaging, and hospital flow. Tube removal was assessed daily by a senior pleural specialist who was blinded to allocation, using predefined criteria: minimal residual effusion on ultrasound without re-accumulation, clinical stability, and improving inflammatory biomarkers. Persistent low-volume output alone was not used as a reason to keep the tube in, which is a thoughtful design choice.

Narrator: The final analysis included 89 patients: 30 in saline lavage, 30 in saline lavage plus I E T, and 29 in I E T alone. Median age was 62 years, 73 percent were male, nearly 80 percent had community-acquired pleural infection, and about one-third had empyema with macroscopic pus. Baseline characteristics were broadly similar, although with a sample this small, modest imbalances always deserve caution.

Guest: The headline result was clear. Saline lavage alone prolonged drainage compared with either I E T-containing strategy. Median drainage duration was 4.0 days with saline lavage, 3.0 days with I E T alone, and 3.0 days with saline lavage plus I E T. The global comparison was significant, with a Kruskal-Wallis p value of 0.0058. Pairwise comparisons were also significant for saline lavage versus I E T alone, p equals 0.01, and saline lavage versus saline plus I E T, p equals 0.01. There was no significant difference between I E T alone and the combined saline plus I E T arm, p equals 0.24.

Narrator: So the hypothesis that saline lavage would improve I E T performance was not supported. And saline lavage alone did not look like a substitute for enzyme therapy. A devil's advocate question is whether a one-day median difference is clinically meaningful.

Guest: That is the key question. For an individual patient, one day may or may not be decisive. But the direction matters. Saline lavage did not reduce drainage duration, did not reduce length of stay, and did not clearly reduce additional procedures. If a protocol adds procedural time, tube manipulation, and workflow complexity, the standard should be more than, can it be done? The standard should be, does it add value? In this trial, routine saline lavage did not clear that bar.

Narrator: Secondary outcomes were consistent with that interpretation. By day 2, all three groups reduced effusion size on chest radiography by roughly 30 percent from baseline, with no significant difference. By day 5, I E T alone was associated with greater radiographic resolution than either saline lavage or combination therapy, with a median residual effusion of zero in the quantile regression analysis and an interaction p value of 0.0026.

Guest: For additional interventions, there was a numerical trend favoring I E T alone, although it was not statistically significant. Additional chest tubes were required in 26.7 percent of the saline lavage group, 10 percent of the saline plus I E T group, and 6.9 percent of the I E T group. Surgery was uncommon: zero in saline lavage, 6.7 percent in saline plus I E T, and zero in I E T alone. Only two patients required VATS debridement, both in the combination group. But the trial was not powered for these endpoints, so these are signals rather than definitive estimates.

Narrator: Length of stay was 8 days with saline lavage versus 6 days in both I E T-containing groups, p equals 0.29. Infection-related mortality at 3 months was low: one patient in saline lavage, one in the combination arm, and none in I E T alone. Adverse events were rare and similar numerically, with one event in each group. The notable major event was one hemothorax in the saline plus I E T group requiring VATS.

Guest: Methodologically, there are several strengths. The comparator was clinically relevant. The trial used randomization, blinded outcome adjudication, predefined tube removal criteria, and intention-to-treat analysis. It also studied a realistic pleural workflow: ultrasound-guided tubes, aggressive initial aspiration, antibiotics, and daily reassessment.

Narrator: But there are limitations. The sample size was modest, the trial involved two centers in one country, and it was not powered for many outcomes clinicians care about most, such as surgery, mortality, treatment failure, or quality of life. The sample size calculation targeted drainage duration, especially the comparison of saline plus I E T versus I E T alone. The authors explicitly note that the trial was not powered to assess non-inferiority.

Guest: Another limitation is the enzyme regimen. They used urokinase plus D-Nase, given concurrently once daily for up to 6 days. That differs from the MIST-2 style regimen of tissue plasminogen activator plus D-Nase, often administered sequentially twice daily for 3 days. Many centers use local variations anyway, but it means the results should not be overextended to every tPA-D-Nase protocol.

Narrator: The saline protocol also matters. This was once-daily manual instillation with immediate aspiration until clear. It was not the irrigation approach used in the Pleural Irrigation Trial, and it was not continuous large-volume lavage. So SCOPE argues against this specific routine lavage strategy in an optimized I E T-based workflow. It does not prove that every possible saline strategy is ineffective.

Guest: That distinction is important. Prior saline irrigation data looked more favorable, but often against chest tube drainage alone, without I E T. SCOPE asks the more relevant question for contemporary IP and pleural services: if enzyme therapy is available and drainage is optimized, does saline add anything? The answer here is no.

Narrator: For clinical translation, the message is pragmatic. In an adult with C P P E or empyema who is eligible for intrapleural enzyme therapy, this study does not support replacing I E T with saline lavage. It also does not support adding routine saline lavage before enzyme therapy when the chest tube is placed under ultrasound guidance, initial aspiration is optimized, and the patient is reassessed daily.

Guest: Saline may still have a selective role. If fibrinolytics are contraindicated or unavailable, or if the immediate problem is tube obstruction by thick material, clinicians may still individualize flushing or lavage. But that is different from making lavage a default adjunct to I E T. The trial also reinforces a broader point: pleural drainage quality is not just the drug. It is tube position, imaging guidance, initial evacuation, reassessment, and a clear endpoint for tube removal.

Narrator: Another devil's advocate point is generalizability. These were pleural medicine services using ultrasound, small-bore tubes, and daily expert reassessment. Would the result look the same in lower-volume settings?

Guest: Maybe not, but that cuts both ways. If optimized drainage leaves little room for saline to help, then a less optimized system probably needs better drainage processes first, not automatically another lavage protocol. Standardizing tube placement, imaging reassessment, enzyme eligibility, and escalation pathways may be the higher-yield intervention.

Narrator: So the synthesis is this: SCOPE is a small but clinically focused randomized trial. Saline lavage alone was associated with longer chest tube duration than I E T. Adding saline lavage to urokinase plus D-Nase did not meaningfully improve drainage duration, radiographic outcomes, length of stay, need for additional procedures, mortality, or adverse events compared with I E T alone.

Guest: The findings should not be oversold as a universal ban on all saline approaches. But for routine use in I E T-eligible pleural infection, especially in centers already performing ultrasound-guided drainage and daily reassessment, the evidence favors keeping the workflow simple: antibiotics, well-positioned drainage, appropriate enzyme therapy, and timely escalation when needed. Saline lavage remains a selective tool, not a default add-on.

Narrator: That wraps this episode of InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. The SCOPE trial gives pleural clinicians a useful reminder that biologically plausible procedural add-ons still need to prove clinical value. For this strategy, the evidence supports intrapleural enzyme therapy as the preferred intrapleural approach and does not support routine saline lavage in addition to, or in place of, enzyme therapy.
