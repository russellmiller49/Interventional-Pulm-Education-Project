Narrator: Welcome to Interventional Pulm dot com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we’re looking at Addala and colleagues, published in CHEST in 2025, examining discordant pleural fluid exudates: specifically, exudative effusions where pleural fluid protein and L D H do not move in the same direction.

Guest: Right, and this is a deceptively practical paper. Pleural fluid interpretation often starts with the binary question: transudate or exudate? But in real pleural practice, the chemistry is often messier than that. You get a fluid that meets exudative criteria, but protein is high and L D H is low, or L D H is high and protein is low. The authors ask whether that pattern is just biochemical noise, or whether it points toward a different diagnostic neighborhood.

Narrator: The study design is straightforward: a retrospective cohort from a UK tertiary pleural center, covering pleural fluid samples from 2015 through 2017. They began with 995 pleural fluid samples. After excluding samples with incomplete protein or L D H data and excluding transudates, they analyzed 715 exudative effusions. The flow diagram on page four is useful here: 486 were concordant exudates, meaning both protein and L D H were high, and 229 were discordant.

Guest: So the headline is that discordance was common. Thirty-two percent of exudative pleural effusions were discordant. Of those discordant cases, 63 percent were protein-high, L D H-low, and 37 percent were L D H-high, protein-low. That alone is clinically relevant, because a third of exudates falling into this gray zone is not a rare edge case.

Narrator: They defined low protein as less than 30 grams per liter and low L D H as less than 170 international units per liter. Then they compared diagnoses between discordant and concordant groups, using chi-square testing, odds ratios, and multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age and sex.

Guest: And the diagnostic pattern is the core of the paper. Compared with concordant exudates, discordant exudates were more likely to be associated with fluid overload, benign asbestos-related pleural effusion, and ICU-associated effusions. Fluid overload was 10 percent in discordant effusions versus 2 percent in concordant effusions, with an adjusted odds ratio of 5.37. BAPE was 14 percent versus 9 percent, adjusted odds ratio 1.88. ICU-associated effusion was 9 percent versus 3 percent, adjusted odds ratio 3.32.

Narrator: On the other side, malignant pleural effusion and pleural infection were less frequent in the discordant group. Malignancy was 34 percent in discordant effusions versus 42 percent in concordant effusions, adjusted odds ratio 0.57. Pleural infection, defined as complex parapneumonic effusion or empyema, was 6 percent versus 16 percent, adjusted odds ratio 0.39. Simple parapneumonic effusion did not differ significantly.

Guest: The practical interpretation is not “discordant means benign.” That would be a dangerous oversimplification. One-third of discordant effusions were still malignant, and in the protein-high, L D H-low subgroup, malignancy was 41 percent, basically similar to the concordant group. So the clinician should not see discordance and abandon cytology, pleural imaging, or biopsy if the pretest probability for malignancy remains high.

Narrator: That is a key nuance. The subgroup analysis is probably the most clinically interesting part of the paper. In protein-high, L D H-low effusions, pleural infection was very uncommon: 1 percent, compared with 16 percent in concordant effusions. That makes biological sense. If L D H reflects cellular turnover and inflammatory burden, a truly low L D H is not the biochemical signature one expects from empyema or complex pleural infection.

Guest: Exactly. But again, not a rule-out test in isolation. If the patient has fever, loculation, low pH, low glucose, organisms, or frankly purulent fluid, nobody should be reassured by one component of the chemistry. But for the undifferentiated outpatient pleural referral with high protein and low L D H, infection may move lower on the working differential, while malignancy, BAPE, and chronic inflammatory or fibrotic pleural processes remain on the table.

Narrator: In the L D H-high, protein-low subgroup, malignancy was less common at 21 percent, and ICU-associated effusions were notably enriched at 18 percent, with an odds ratio over 6 compared with concordant exudates. Fluid overload was also increased in both discordant subgroups.

Guest: That fluid overload signal is probably one of the most actionable findings. Light’s criteria are intentionally sensitive for exudates, but clinicians have long recognized that diuresed heart failure can look exudative. This study is not simply re-litigating Light’s criteria, but it supports a workflow point: when the exudate is discordant, especially in a patient with plausible cardiac, renal, or hepatic physiology, actively revisit volume status. Consider serum-pleural albumin gradients, serum protein gradients, BNP or NT-proBNP when appropriate, echocardiographic context, medication timing, and the trajectory after diuresis.

Narrator: But here’s the devil’s advocate question: is discordance truly a distinct biological entity, or partly a threshold artifact?

Guest: That’s the right critique. The thresholds are absolute cutoffs tied to local laboratory ranges: protein less than 30 grams per liter and L D H less than 170 international units per liter. A fluid with protein of 31 and L D H of 165 is classified differently from one with protein of 29 and L D H of 175, even though clinically they may be close cousins. So this is not a precision diagnostic classifier. It is a pattern-recognition study.

Narrator: And the retrospective design matters. Final diagnoses were derived from the electronic record and pleural multidisciplinary review, which is a strength compared with simple coding data. But retrospective adjudication can still miss dual diagnoses. A patient may have mild heart failure and malignancy, or ICU-related capillary leak and infection evolving over time.

Guest: Plus the setting matters. This was a tertiary UK pleural center with low TB prevalence. The paper itself notes that TB and connective tissue disease numbers were small, so the absence of a significant association there should not be overinterpreted. A center with more TB, more empyema, or a different referral pathway might see different distributions.

Narrator: Another limitation is that serum protein and serum L D H were infrequently available. That means the study cannot fully address how discordance maps onto traditional Light’s criteria ratios, or whether albumin and cholesterol-based approaches would reclassify some of these exudates as pseudoexudates.

Guest: That is important, because the clinical problem is not just naming discordance. It is deciding what to do next. Does this patient need thoracoscopy? Repeat cytology? Cross-sectional imaging? A trial of diuresis and follow-up imaging? Occupational asbestos exposure review? Pleural infection pathway? The paper does not give a decision rule with sensitivity, specificity, or likelihood ratios. It gives a diagnostic pattern that should modify pretest probability.

Narrator: For interventional pulmonologists, this has several workflow implications. First, look at protein and L D H separately rather than stopping at “exudate.” Second, in discordant exudates, deliberately ask whether the patient fits a fluid-overload phenotype, especially if imaging and clinical context are not strongly malignant or infectious. Third, in protein-high, L D H-low effusions, keep malignancy on the table, but recognize that complex pleural infection was rare in this cohort. Fourth, in the right exposure context, BAPE deserves attention, particularly because asbestos-related benign pleuritis can mimic more concerning pleural disease and may require longitudinal surveillance.

Guest: And from a procedural standpoint, this may help prioritize the next step. A concordant exudate with high L D H, suspicious pleural thickening, and negative cytology may push quickly toward image-guided biopsy or thoracoscopy. A discordant exudate in a patient with clear volume overload and bland imaging may justify a more conservative diagnostic sequence, provided follow-up is tight and the malignant differential is not being ignored.

Narrator: The safety issue is avoiding both extremes. One extreme is reflexively labeling every exudate as malignant or infectious and escalating to invasive diagnostics too quickly. The other is dismissing discordant exudates as pseudoexudates and missing malignancy. The study argues for neither. It argues for more careful biochemical phenotyping.

Guest: The most useful phrase may be: discordance is not a diagnosis, but it is a signal. It tells the pleural clinician to re-weight the differential. It may point toward fluid overload, ICU-associated physiology, or asbestos-related pleural disease. It may make complex infection less likely when L D H is low. But it does not replace imaging, cytology, microbiology, pH and glucose interpretation, pleural biopsy strategy, or clinical judgment.

Narrator: So the bottom line: Addala and colleagues show that discordant pleural fluid exudates are common, accounting for about one-third of exudative effusions in this cohort, and they carry a different diagnostic profile than concordant exudates. The paper is not practice-changing in the sense of mandating a new algorithm, but it is practice-sharpening. It encourages clinicians to look beyond the exudate label and use the directionality of protein and L D H to guide a more nuanced diagnostic pathway.

Guest: And the next step for the field would be prospective, multicenter validation, ideally incorporating serum gradients, albumin, cholesterol, NT-proBNP, longitudinal diagnosis, and center-level variation in TB, infection, malignancy, and asbestos exposure. That would help determine whether discordance can become part of a formal diagnostic pathway rather than an interesting biochemical observation.
