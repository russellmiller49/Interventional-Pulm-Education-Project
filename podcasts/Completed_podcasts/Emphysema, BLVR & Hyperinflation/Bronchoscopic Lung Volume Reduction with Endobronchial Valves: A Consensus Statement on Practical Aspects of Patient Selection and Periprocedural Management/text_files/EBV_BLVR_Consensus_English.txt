Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. Today we’re discussing Wahidi and colleagues, published in Respiration in 2026: “Bronchoscopic Lung Volume Reduction with Endobronchial Valves: A Consensus Statement on Practical Aspects of Patient Selection and Periprocedural Management.”

Guest: And this is a useful paper because it lives in the gap between randomized trial eligibility criteria and the actual BLVR clinic. The pivotal studies tell us valves can improve lung function, dyspnea, and functional status in appropriately selected patients with severe hyperinflated emphysema and absent collateral ventilation. But the day-to-day questions are messier: What about high oxygen requirement? Borderline hypercapnia? Pulmonary hypertension? Prior pleurodesis? A left upper lobe target with a high pneumothorax risk? That is where this consensus statement is trying to help.

Narrator: Right. And important framing up front: this is not a randomized trial, and it is not a formal GRADE-based society guideline. It is a modified Delphi consensus statement from an international expert panel. Eleven BLVR experts participated, recommendations were developed after topic selection and literature review, and consensus required more than 80 percent agreement.

Guest: The methods matter. They started with 23 recommendations, went through three Delphi rounds, and ended with 21 recommendations reaching consensus. One topic failed to reach consensus, which was pulmonary rehabilitation, and two areas were judged to have insufficient evidence for a recommendation: staged valve placement over two procedures and sequential bilateral BLVR. That alone tells you the paper is not just rubber-stamping conventional practice. There were areas where the panel explicitly said, “We do not have enough here.”

Narrator: The figures make that visible. Figure 1 lays out the consensus workflow, from expert recruitment and practice survey through literature review and three voting rounds. Figure 2 is especially helpful because it shows where agreement was immediate and where it was harder won. You can see the friction around pulmonary rehab, staged BLVR, perfusion testing, and some of the physiologic risk thresholds.

Guest: The biggest conceptual message is that the recommendations are guidance, not a rigid contraindication checklist. The authors explicitly acknowledge that randomized trial criteria were often conservative and designed for research cleanliness. In clinic, a patient with one abnormal domain may still be a candidate after optimization and shared decision-making, while a patient who is borderline across several domains may be a poor candidate even if no single value is catastrophic.

Narrator: Let’s start with preprocedure patient selection. The first recommendation is multidisciplinary review when local expertise is available: COPD, LVRS, transplant, radiology, and IP. That is intuitive, but the authors are careful not to make it mandatory. They acknowledge the potential benefits, like identifying patients better suited for LVRS or transplant, or catching nodules, bronchiectasis, diaphragm issues, or comorbid disease, but they also say outcomes data proving the value of a formal committee are limited.

Guest: That is a reasonable balance. A high-quality unilateral review may be sufficient in some settings, but for borderline BLVR candidates, a multidisciplinary severe emphysema conference can prevent tunnel vision. The devil’s advocate question is whether this recommendation improves outcomes, or simply reflects what high-volume programs already do. The paper does not prove the former.

Narrator: Then come the physiologic thresholds. The panel suggests caution when a patient needs at least four liters per minute of oxygen at rest to maintain adequate saturation. They recommend against proceeding when PaCO2 exceeds 55 millimeters of mercury despite optimal medical therapy and nocturnal ventilation. They suggest caution with L V E F less than 40 percent, recommend against proceeding in decompensated heart failure, suggest caution with moderate pulmonary hypertension, and recommend against proceeding when pulmonary hypertension is severe.

Guest: These are probably among the most clinically useful parts of the paper, but they are also among the least evidence-secure. Many of these thresholds come from trial exclusions and physiologic concern rather than robust BLVR-specific risk modeling. The recurring logic is tolerance of sudden decompensation, especially pneumothorax. A patient with severe hypercapnia, severe pulmonary hypertension, or decompensated heart failure may not tolerate the complication profile that otherwise makes BLVR acceptable.

Narrator: The BMI recommendation is also pragmatic. They recommend against excluding patients just because BMI is high or low, but they advise caution at extremes, like BMI below 18 or above 40, or when the body habitus brings comorbidities such as obesity hypoventilation, malnutrition, or pulmonary cachexia.

Guest: And that fits the overall tone of the document. Do not treat trial cutoffs as sacred, but do not ignore the physiology. A very high BMI may blunt symptomatic benefit or complicate anesthesia and chest tube management. A very low BMI may be a frailty marker. Neither should automatically close the door, but both should change the risk conversation.

Narrator: Moving to emphysema phenotype and target selection, the panel says to evaluate both heterogeneous and homogeneous centrilobular or panlobular emphysema. They suggest avoiding routine BLVR in predominantly paraseptal emphysema or giant bullae.

Guest: That is a subtle but important point. Homogeneous emphysema should not be dismissed categorically, but expectations and selection differ. The IMPACT trial space is relevant here, and the paper notes that homogeneous disease trials used a higher residual volume threshold. For giant bullae, the paper leans toward bullectomy evaluation, with valve treatment limited to highly selected nonoperative candidates. For predominantly paraseptal disease, the concern is not just pneumothorax; it is also whether the target lobe will actually deflate and whether adjacent compensatory hyperinflation will erase benefit.

Narrator: The target-lobe recommendations are where this consensus statement becomes very procedure-facing. The authors suggest quantitative C T for all candidates to estimate fissure integrity, lobar destruction, and lobar volumes. They suggest potential targets should have extensive emphysema, defined as at least 50 percent low attenuation at minus 910 Hounsfield units, or at least 20 percent at minus 950 Hounsfield units, adjacent to fissures that are at least 80 percent intact.

Guest: And they do not stop with fissures and destruction score. If there are multiple potential targets or homogeneous distribution, they suggest considering perfusion testing. They advise avoiding treatment of a lobe in the lung with clearly lower emphysema burden or greater perfusion. And for choosing the primary target, they recommend integrating emphysema destruction, lobe volume, perfusion, and disqualifying findings like nodules, infection, large bullae, or predominantly paraseptal disease.

Narrator: The practical translation is that target selection is not “pick the worst-looking lobe and go.” It is a composite decision: enough destruction, low perfusion, adequate lobar volume, intact fissures, no problematic nodules or infection, no prior ipsilateral lobectomy or pleurodesis, and no obvious right-left mismatch that would put the better lung at risk.

Guest: Exactly. And the authors make a notable recommendation about pneumothorax: do not exclude patients purely because you think they have an increased pneumothorax risk. That may sound counterintuitive until you unpack it. The most reliable pneumothorax risk factor is also the prerequisite for success: an intact fissure and absence of collateral ventilation. Pneumothorax is often a marker that volume reduction is happening.

Narrator: But pneumothorax remains the central safety issue. The paper cites post-BLVR pneumothorax rates of 12 to 29 percent in trials that selected collateral-ventilation-negative patients, a 2017 meta-analysis with a relative risk of 9.65 versus control, and a U.S. population-level estimate around 26 percent in real-world admissions. So the point is not to minimize pneumothorax. It is to select patients who can survive it.

Guest: That is the key phrase: select patients who can tolerate the predictable complication profile. If a patient would be devastated by a pneumothorax, the answer is not to reassure yourself that the pneumothorax risk is low. The answer may be that BLVR is not the right therapy.

Narrator: Procedurally, the panel recommends direct physiologic assessment for collateral ventilation before all right-sided valve placements, and before left-sided valve placement when fissure integrity is below 95 percent. They are essentially saying quantitative C T is necessary but not always sufficient.

Guest: And the data they summarize support that. Balloon occlusion testing improved prediction of meaningful volume loss, with one cited observational study showing significant volume loss in 58 percent of lobes without collateral ventilation versus 8 percent with detectable collateral ventilation. But the authors also acknowledge the messiness of Chartis-type assessments: airway collapse, low-flow states, mucus, anatomy, inconclusive testing, and false positives. So again, it is not algorithm worship. It is integrated interpretation.

Narrator: Then comes the three-night admission recommendation after BLVR. This is one of the more prescriptive recommendations, and it is hard to argue with. Most pneumothoraces in trials occurred within three days, many required chest tubes, and these patients have limited reserve.

Guest: Ambulatory BLVR sounds attractive until you imagine the wrong patient developing a tension physiology pneumothorax outside a monitored setting. The paper’s recommendation for at least three nights is not just tradition. It reflects timing of complications and the severity of the population.

Narrator: Postprocedure management is also covered. The panel suggests follow-up at four to six weeks, three to six months, and 12 months, assessing clinical and functional status, radiographic response, and complications. Then they separate nonresponse patterns. Subtotal atelectasis without clinical improvement by three months should trigger C T and consideration of bronchoscopic inspection with revision or removal if indicated. Complete atelectasis without clinical improvement should trigger P F T’s, C T, cardiac evaluation, and other clinically guided testing.

Guest: That distinction is useful. If there is no atelectasis, think valve malposition, undersizing, missed segment, malfunction, granulation, or occult collateral ventilation. If there is complete atelectasis but no clinical benefit, then the valve may have done its mechanical job, and the problem may be compensatory hyperinflation, cardiac disease, pulmonary vascular disease, fibrosis, deconditioning, or another limitation.

Narrator: And if the patient initially responds, then later loses clinical and radiographic benefit, the panel suggests C T and revision bronchoscopy to evaluate malposition, malfunction, granulation tissue, infection, or another cause.

Guest: That is clinically important because BLVR is not always a one-and-done intervention. Revision bronchoscopy is part of the longitudinal care model. Patients should know before the first valve is placed that later inspection, revision, temporary removal, or replacement may be needed.

Narrator: The pulmonary rehab section is interesting because it did not reach consensus. The authors describe conflicting interpretations: rehab is safe, guideline-supported in COPD, and often embedded in severe emphysema care, but access barriers are real, and the available BLVR-specific evidence did not clearly show improved postprocedure endurance based on timing. A multicenter randomized trial found no difference between rehab before BLVR, rehab after BLVR, or no rehab. A post hoc VENT analysis showed a mean six-minute walk improvement of 14 meters, below the 26-meter minimum clinically important difference.

Guest: That is a nice example of a mature consensus process. Not everything has to become a recommendation. The danger would be turning a good general COPD intervention into a rigid BLVR gatekeeping requirement when the evidence is not there and access is uneven.

Narrator: The same applies to staged BLVR and sequential bilateral BLVR. The paper notes insufficient evidence for a recommendation. Staged treatment has signals that it might reduce pneumothorax risk, with retrospective studies reporting pneumothorax rates of 7 and 12 percent, but no comparative randomized evidence and obvious tradeoffs: longer cumulative hospitalization, more procedures, and cost. Sequential bilateral BLVR is even less settled, with only small retrospective experience and higher complication signals in some reports.

Guest: One additional provocative point is low intraprocedural F I O two. The paper notes a retrospective before-and-after study associating low F I O two, mean 0.29, with pneumothorax of 7 percent versus 31 percent with high F I O two, mean 0.95. That is physiologically interesting because of absorption atelectasis, but the paper appropriately treats it as hypothesis-generating. It should not become dogma based on one observational signal.

Narrator: So what does this article add for the IP audience?

Guest: It gives a structured, clinically usable framework for the parts of BLVR that are hardest to standardize: borderline candidacy, target selection, procedural confirmation of collateral ventilation, inpatient monitoring, and postprocedure troubleshooting. The article is strongest when it acknowledges uncertainty. It tells us where expert centers converge, but also where evidence is thin.

Narrator: The limitations are equally important. This is expert consensus, not prospective validation of thresholds. Many recommendations are anchored in trial eligibility criteria, retrospective series, small observational studies, device-era assumptions, and high-volume expertise. There are also device-related conflicts among some authors, which is common in procedural fields but still worth keeping in mind.

Guest: The practical takeaway is not “follow these 21 statements mechanically.” It is more nuanced: use them to make BLVR programs more consistent, to sharpen the risk-benefit discussion, and to avoid both excessive nihilism and excessive enthusiasm. A patient with emphysema is not a lobe attached to a fissure score. The decision is physiologic, radiographic, procedural, and longitudinal.

Narrator: Well said. For InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review, the bottom line is this: Wahidi and colleagues provide a high-yield consensus map for BLVR with endobronchial valves. It reinforces quantitative C T, physiologic collateral ventilation assessment, careful target selection, three-night admission, and structured follow-up, while openly identifying unresolved areas like pulmonary rehab requirements, staged valve placement, sequential bilateral treatment, and strategies to reduce pneumothorax.

Guest: And for IP clinicians, that is probably the right posture: disciplined selection, procedural humility, and a plan for what happens after the valves go in.
