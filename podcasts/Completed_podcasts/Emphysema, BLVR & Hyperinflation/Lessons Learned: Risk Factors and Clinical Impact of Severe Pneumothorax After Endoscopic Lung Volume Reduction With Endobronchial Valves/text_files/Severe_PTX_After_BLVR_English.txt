arrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. This episode discusses Brock and colleagues, published in CHEST in 2025, on risk factors and clinical impact of severe pneumothorax after endoscopic lung volume reduction with endobronchial valves. Pneumothorax is one of the defining complications of valve-based BLVR, but this paper asks a more granular question: which pneumothoraces are clinically severe, and which patients are at risk for that severe phenotype?
Guest: That distinction is important because pneumothorax after valve placement is not a single clinical entity. A small, stable pneumothorax can be manageable and may even accompany effective target lobe collapse. A prolonged air leak requiring valve removal, ICU-level care, or reintervention is a very different outcome. The paper tries to separate those categories rather than treating all pneumothoraces as equivalent.
Narrator: This was a large single-center retrospective analysis of 532 patients who underwent valve placement. Among them, 102 developed pneumothorax. The authors stratified pneumothorax into three severity groups: mild, meaning no chest tube was required; moderate, meaning a chest tube was placed but valves remained in place; and severe, meaning prolonged air leak requiring chest tube placement and subsequent valve removal.
Guest: The overall pneumothorax rate was 19%. Among patients with pneumothorax, about 30% were mild, about 30% were moderate, and nearly 40% were severe. As expected, pneumothorax was more common after upper lobe treatment than lower lobe treatment, 31.3% versus 11.3%, and was associated with higher fissure integrity.
Narrator: The key contribution is the analysis of severe pneumothorax. The strongest signals were valve placement in the left upper lobe and a larger, more hyperinflated target lobe volume. The left upper lobe accounted for more than half of severe cases. Clinically, severe pneumothorax was associated with a much harder course: hospital stay around 18 days versus about 10 days for the other pneumothorax groups, with higher rates of respiratory insufficiency and other complications.
Guest: The reimplantation data may be the most practice-informing part of the paper. Among patients who had valves removed for severe pneumothorax, the risk of repeat pneumothorax after reimplantation was 42.9%. If the initial management required sequential complete removal because partial valve removal failed to stop the leak, the repeat pneumothorax risk after reimplantation increased to 75%.
Narrator: Those are numbers that change the tone of shared decision-making. Reimplantation after a severe pneumothorax is not just restarting the original treatment plan. It is a second exposure to a complication risk that may be substantially higher than the initial baseline risk, especially in patients whose first event required complete valve removal.
Guest: Methodologically, though, this remains a retrospective single-center study. The authors are transparent about several limitations. Fissure integrity was assessed by radiologists rather than by a quantitative CT algorithm, and the full baseline characteristics of the non-pneumothorax cohort were not fully analyzed in a way that would allow a robust predictive model. So the study is very useful for generating risk signals, but it does not give a bedside calculator for severe pneumothorax.
Narrator: The single-center nature cuts both ways. A high-volume BLVR center may have consistent protocols for patient selection, post-procedure monitoring, chest tube management, and valve removal. That improves internal consistency. But the results may not translate perfectly to centers with different thresholds for chest tube placement, different admission protocols, or different comfort levels with partial versus complete valve removal.
Guest: There is also a key devil's advocate issue: the left upper lobe is not a modifiable risk factor in the simple sense. It is often the correct target. If the LUL is the most destroyed lobe, has intact fissures, and is driving hyperinflation, avoiding treatment solely because the risk of severe pneumothorax is higher could deny a patient meaningful benefit.
Narrator: Exactly. The actionable lesson is not "never treat the left upper lobe." It is to refine consent, monitoring, and contingency planning. A patient with a very large, hyperinflated LUL target should understand that the expected benefit may be paired with a higher risk of a severe, prolonged, resource-intensive pneumothorax. That discussion is different from quoting an overall pneumothorax rate.
Guest: The loss to follow-up in the severe pneumothorax group that did not undergo reimplantation also matters. The study also reported that more than 73% were unavailable for assessment. That makes it difficult to know the true functional consequences of severe pneumothorax followed by no reimplantation. Some patients may have derived partial benefit; others may have had no durable benefit and significant harm. The available data cannot cleanly separate those trajectories.
Narrator: Still, the clinical impact is hard to ignore. Severe pneumothorax was not just a radiographic event. It was associated with prolonged hospitalization, respiratory insufficiency, escalation of respiratory support, and one death. That supports the authors' argument that future BLVR studies should report pneumothorax by severity rather than simply giving a binary yes-or-no rate.
Guest: For procedural planning, this paper reinforces several practical points. First, post-valve monitoring needs to be robust because the complication can be clinically significant. Second, reimplantation after severe pneumothorax should be approached as a high-risk decision, not as a routine redo. Third, future research should focus less on predicting any pneumothorax and more on predicting severe pneumothorax.
Narrator: That is an important shift. Any pneumothorax may be partly linked to therapeutic atelectasis. Severe pneumothorax is the outcome that threatens the patient's course and may erase the intended benefit. Predicting the severe subgroup is more clinically meaningful than predicting the entire pneumothorax category.
Guest: The synthesis is that Brock and colleagues give the IP community a useful severity framework and practical reimplantation data. The LUL and large target lobe volume appear to identify patients at higher risk for severe events, but the retrospective design limits causal certainty. The data should improve risk counseling and study design more than it should restrict appropriate candidates from therapy.
Narrator: For IP clinicians, the paper supports a more precise consent conversation: not just "pneumothorax can happen," but "severe pneumothorax can lead to prolonged hospitalization, valve removal, and a substantial recurrence risk if reimplantation is attempted." That closes this episode of InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. Thanks for listening.
