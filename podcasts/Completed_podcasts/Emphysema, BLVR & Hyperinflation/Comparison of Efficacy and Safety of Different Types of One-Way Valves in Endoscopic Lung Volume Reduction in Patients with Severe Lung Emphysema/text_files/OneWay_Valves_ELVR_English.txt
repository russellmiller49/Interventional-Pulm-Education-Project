Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we are discussing Sgarbossa and colleagues, published in Respiration in 2025, comparing the efficacy and safety of different one-way valves for endoscopic lung volume reduction in severe emphysema. The question is straightforward but clinically relevant: do outcomes differ between Zephyr endobronchial valves and the Spiration Valve System when used in real-world BLVR practice?
Guest: This is a useful comparison because the evidence base has historically been built around separate pivotal studies rather than direct head-to-head data. Zephyr EBV has data from trials such as LIBERATE, while SVS has data from EMPROVE. In actual BLVR programs, valve choice is often driven by airway anatomy, operator familiarity, availability, and the perceived ability to obtain a stable seal.
Narrator: The study used a German lung emphysema registry across three centers. It was prospective and observational, not randomized. The investigators included 153 patients undergoing ELVR: 99 treated with EBV and 54 treated with SVS. Patients were assessed for collateral ventilation using Chartis or CT analysis, consistent with standard valve selection practice.
Guest: The baseline characteristics were well matched. There were no significant differences in age, BMI, emphysema score, baseline pulmonary function, 6-minute walk distance, or quality-of-life measures such as SGRQ and CAT. The median FEV1 was around 27% to 29% predicted, and residual volume was around 240% to 250% predicted. So this was a typical severe hyperinflation BLVR population.
Narrator: At 3-to-6-month follow-up, both valve groups improved compared with their own baselines in FEV1 and residual volume. But when the magnitude of improvement was compared between groups, the differences were not statistically significant. Median FEV1 improvement was identical at 100 mL in both groups. Median residual volume reduction was -0.5 liters with SVS and -0.8 liters with EBV, with P = 0.517. Changes in 6-minute walk distance and SGRQ were also not significantly different between groups.
Guest: Safety looked broadly similar as well. Pneumothorax was the most frequent adverse event, as expected, occurring in 9% of the SVS group and 12% of the EBV group. AECOPD and pneumonia rates were comparable. There was one death in each group within 90 days, corresponding to about 1% in the EBV group and about 2% in the SVS group, with the cause of death in the SVS patient not determined from the available data.
Narrator: On the surface, that sounds like the valves are essentially interchangeable. But this is where the study design matters. This was not a randomized trial. Valve selection was left to the proceduralist, and the authors note that anatomy could influence the choice. SVS may be preferred for short, round bronchi, whereas EBV may be preferred for longer, straighter airways. That means treatment allocation was partly anatomy-based.
Guest: And anatomy is not a trivial confounder in BLVR. Airway geometry affects seal, stability, migration risk, sizing, and potentially the degree of target lobe volume reduction. Even if the baseline table is well matched for physiology and symptoms, the airway-level variables that influenced valve choice may still influence outcomes.
Narrator: The unequal group sizes also matter: 99 EBV patients versus 54 SVS patients. That suggests a center-level preference or availability pattern. It does not invalidate the results, but it reminds the reader that this is pragmatic registry evidence rather than an equipoise-driven trial.
Guest: Missing follow-up is another important limitation. The SVS arm lost 13 of 54 patients before follow-up analysis, which is about 24%. The EBV arm lost 13 of 99 patients, about 13%. Differential loss to follow-up can bias outcomes if patients with poor response or complications are less likely to return.
Narrator: There is also a subtle signal in the within-group analyses. The EBV group achieved statistically significant improvements in DLCO, 6-minute walk distance, and mMRC score from baseline, whereas the SVS group did not reach statistical significance for those parameters. The authors reasonably suggest this may reflect smaller sample size in the SVS group, but it is still worth noting.
Guest: The biggest missing physiologic endpoint is quantitative target lobe volume reduction. Without TLVR, it is hard to know whether the valves produced equivalent lobar collapse. Two devices might generate similar mean FEV1 changes while achieving different degrees of atelectasis, different durability, or different lobar mechanics. The registry also did not track procedural time or length of stay, both of which would be relevant to real-world device selection.
Narrator: Conflicts of interest are also part of the context. The study reports no direct funding, but several authors disclosed honoraria or travel expenses from Pulmonx and Olympus. That is common in device-based procedural fields and does not invalidate the work, but it should be interpreted transparently.
Guest: So, does this article change practice? Probably not in a dramatic way. It does not show one valve is superior. Instead, it provides reassuring comparative evidence that, in these centers, both systems produced similar clinical and physiologic outcomes when selected by proceduralists.
Narrator: That may actually support current practice. The practical question in BLVR is rarely "which valve is better in the abstract?" It is "which valve will seal this patient's airway anatomy most reliably and safely?" This registry supports an anatomy-guided, patient-specific approach rather than a brand-based hierarchy.
Guest: For implementation, the study also argues for maintaining familiarity with both systems when possible. If each device has an anatomic niche, a mature BLVR program may benefit from having both tools available. But the evidence remains limited by nonrandomized assignment, differential follow-up, and missing TLVR data.
Narrator: The synthesis is that Sgarbossa and colleagues provide useful head-to-head registry data showing no statistically significant efficacy or safety difference between EBV and SVS in a severe emphysema population selected for valve therapy. The result is reassuring, but not definitive.
Guest: A future randomized or carefully matched comparative study should include quantitative TLVR, durability of response, revision bronchoscopy, valve migration, procedural time, length of stay, patient-reported outcomes, and cost. Until then, the best reading is that valve selection should remain anatomy-driven and program-specific.
Narrator: For IP clinicians, this paper validates the pragmatic approach of choosing the device that best fits the airway, while avoiding overinterpretation that the two systems are proven equivalent in every setting.
