Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we're discussing an education-focused paper from CHEST in 2025, first author New, titled "Virtual Reality Anatomy Trainer Turns Teaching Endobronchial Ultrasound Inside-Out." The study evaluates whether a virtual reality anatomy trainer improves EBUS learning compared with traditional two-dimensional diagrams or a three-dimensional printed model.

Guest: This is a useful question because EBUS requires more than scope handling and needle technique. Trainees have to translate airway anatomy, nodal stations, vascular relationships, and ultrasound images into a three-dimensional mental model. That cognitive mapping is often a bottleneck.

Narrator: The study was a randomized crossover trial with 68 participants across three academic centers, including pulmonary fellows and internal medicine residents. The investigators used two anatomy-focused items from the validated EBUS-STAT on a Simbionix simulator to establish baseline performance.

Guest: Participants were randomized 1:1:1 to a self-paced learning session using one of three approaches: traditional 2D pictures, a physical 3D model, or the VR trainer. After the first session, they were retested. Then the crossover occurred: the two groups that started with traditional methods switched to VR, while the group that started with VR crossed over to one of the traditional methods. They completed a final EBUS-STAT assessment after the second session.

Narrator: They also included the Mental Rotation Test, or MRT, as an objective measure of spatial reasoning. That turns out to be central to the interpretation.

Guest: For the primary performance outcome, all three training methods improved EBUS-STAT scores after the first learning session. For a trainee with an average pretest score around 14.6, the predicted score increased to about 21.6 after using the 2D or 3D models and to 20.6 after using VR.

Narrator: So there was no clear winner on the main score outcome. The mean difference in improvement between VR and traditional methods was -1.0 point, with a confidence interval crossing zero. VR was not superior for immediate EBUS-STAT improvement.

Guest: Correct. Another notable finding was that the second learning session did not add measurable improvement. Scores rose after the first intervention and then plateaued regardless of sequence.

Narrator: But the spatial reasoning result is the most interesting piece. MRT score strongly predicted performance in the traditional learning groups. For every extra point on the MRT, the EBUS-STAT score increased by 0.88 points.

Guest: Exactly. Trainees with stronger mental rotation ability learned more effectively from 2D pictures and 3D models. But after participants used VR, that association disappeared. The VR trainer appeared to mitigate the effect of baseline spatial reasoning.

Narrator: That is the strongest argument for VR in this study. It did not outperform traditional learning overall, but it may have leveled the learning environment for participants who otherwise struggle with mental rotation.

Guest: The qualitative data support that interpretation. Users described the VR trainer as helping them build a mental map from within the airway, rather than requiring them to flip anatomy around in their head. That is a plausible mechanism for reducing cognitive load.

Narrator: Let's look at the design strengths. It was multi-site, randomized, used a validated assessment tool, included a crossover structure, measured spatial reasoning, and collected qualitative data. That is a thoughtful education study.

Guest: Agreed. But the limitations are also familiar. The outcome was simulator performance, not real patient EBUS performance. Skill on a Simbionix task does not necessarily translate to mediastinal staging accuracy, sampling quality, procedure time, complication avoidance, or real-world procedural autonomy.

Narrator: The crossover design is efficient, but the absence of improvement after the second session raises questions. Does one focused anatomy session produce most of the gain? Is there a ceiling effect in the EBUS-STAT items used? Or did the study isolate a micro-skill that improves quickly and then plateaus?

Guest: Those are important questions. The study may be teaching us that any deliberate anatomy training helps early learners, and that modality matters most for learners with lower spatial reasoning. That is a narrower but still useful conclusion.

Narrator: The population was also heterogeneous, ranging from PGY-4 residents to third-year pulmonary and critical care fellows, with EBUS experience from zero cases to more than 25. The authors reported that learner level did not modify the effect, but small subgroup sizes could miss important differences.

Guest: Right. A novice who has never driven an EBUS scope may need anatomy orientation, while a more advanced fellow may need ultrasound interpretation, needle targeting, and staging strategy. Those are different educational problems.

Narrator: The qualitative feedback makes that distinction clear. Participants liked VR for understanding 3D vascular and nodal anatomy, but many pointed out that interpreting the actual ultrasound image remained a separate challenge. Several suggested that the next iteration should include a simulated ultrasound view.

Guest: That seems like the logical next step. EBUS is not just bronchoscopy with anatomy labels. The operator must connect airway location, expected nodal station, vascular landmarks, ultrasound morphology, and needle path in real time. A VR anatomy trainer addresses one piece of that chain.

Narrator: Devil's advocate: if VR did not improve the primary score more than traditional tools, is it worth the cost and implementation complexity?

Guest: That depends on the goal. If the goal is average immediate score improvement, traditional tools performed just as well. If the goal is reducing dependence on innate spatial ability, improving learner engagement, and standardizing anatomy exposure, VR may have value. The study reports that 96% of participants preferred VR after using it, and preference could influence time-on-task and retention, even though this study did not measure long-term outcomes.

Narrator: Another skeptical point is whether institutions should screen trainees with the MRT and assign them to VR.

Guest: That would be premature as a firm recommendation. But the study supports the broader concept of personalized procedural education. Spatial reasoning may identify learners who benefit from immersive 3D tools, while others may do well with conventional materials.

Narrator: For IP training programs, the study reinforces the idea of decomposing complex procedures into micro-skills: anatomy first, then ultrasound interpretation, then needle mechanics, then staging strategy and pathology adequacy.

Guest: Exactly. It also shows that VR is not a magic bullet. It may help with one cognitive task - three-dimensional anatomy mapping - but it does not automatically create procedural competence.

Narrator: The next studies need to look at retention, transfer to clinical performance, time to competency, staging completeness, sample adequacy, and whether VR-assisted curricula reduce faculty teaching burden or improve equity across trainees.

Guest: And they should test more integrated platforms: airway view, ultrasound view, nodal station labeling, vascular anatomy, and realistic needle behavior. That would better reflect the actual procedural task.
