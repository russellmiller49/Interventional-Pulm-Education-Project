Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. This episode looks at Chrissian and colleagues, published in ATS Scholar in 2025, on the impact of pulmonary and critical care fellow participation during advanced diagnostic bronchoscopy. The paper addresses a question every training program has to confront: what is the measurable cost of procedural education in a high-volume bronchoscopy suite?
Guest: And the key point is that the paper is not asking whether fellows should be trained. That answer is obvious. The more useful question is how fellow participation affects duration, safety, diagnostic performance, and resource use, especially for advanced diagnostic bronchoscopy rather than routine airway inspection.
Narrator: The investigators analyzed 628 advanced diagnostic bronchoscopies from a prospectively collected registry at a single large academic center over roughly four years. Cases were compared based on whether a PCCM fellow participated or whether the IP attending performed the case without fellow involvement. Importantly, the trainees were general PCCM fellows rotating on an IP service, not dedicated IP fellows.
Guest: That distinction matters for generalizability. Many academic centers train PCCM fellows in EBUS and other advanced bronchoscopic techniques without having an IP fellowship structure. So the paper is relevant to a common educational model, not just to a niche interventional fellowship environment.
Narrator: The main finding was that fellow involvement prolonged procedure duration. After multivariable adjustment, fellow-involved cases were 7.2 minutes longer on average. When the authors stratified by training year, the pattern was not simply linear. PGY-4 participation added about 5 minutes and was not statistically significant. PGY-6 participation added 7.1 minutes. PGY-5 participation added the most time, with an adjusted increase of 8.8 minutes, corresponding to about a 16% decrease in efficiency compared with faculty-only cases.
Guest: The PGY-5 finding is the most interesting operationally. In one scenario, convex probe EBUS without ROSE, the unadjusted difference for PGY-5 fellows was 14.5 minutes. That suggests the steepest efficiency penalty may occur not with the newest trainee, but with the trainee who is being granted more procedural responsibility for the more technically demanding parts of the case.
Narrator: Safety also changed, at least for minor outcomes. Fellow-involved procedures had higher odds of any complication, with an adjusted odds ratio of 2.0. They were also more likely to be prematurely terminated, with an adjusted odds ratio close to 5. But diagnostic yield and sensitivity for malignancy were similar, and major complications did not differ.
Guest: That context is crucial. The study does not suggest that supervised fellow participation compromised the central diagnostic endpoint or increased major harm. The signal is more about time, interruptions, minor complications, and procedure completion. Those still matter, but they are different from saying trainees make advanced bronchoscopy unsafe or diagnostically inferior.
Narrator: Methodologically, the study has strengths and limitations. The prospective registry is a strength because it improves data capture. But the analysis itself is observational and single-center. Fellow participation was based on availability, not randomization, so selection bias is unavoidable. Cases may have differed in subtle ways that were not fully captured in the model.
Guest: There is also the issue of faculty expertise. The supervising bronchoscopists were experienced interventional pulmonologists. That expertise may have protected diagnostic yield and major safety outcomes despite fellow participation. In a lower-volume program, or with less experienced supervisors, the balance between teaching and safety might look different.
Narrator: Let's play devil's advocate. The average adjusted delay was 7.2 minutes, and the authors' own prespecified threshold for a clinically meaningful delay was 10 minutes. So one could argue that the average effect was statistically significant but not operationally dramatic. If yield and major complications were preserved, perhaps this is simply the expected cost of training.
Guest: That is a fair interpretation at the level of a single case. But block time is cumulative. A 7-to-9-minute penalty across several cases can compress a schedule, increase turnover pressure, and potentially eliminate a procedure slot over a busy day. The higher odds of premature termination are also more difficult to dismiss because an aborted diagnostic procedure has direct implications for patients, scheduling, and downstream cancer care.
Narrator: The PGY-5 signal also gives fellowship directors something actionable. If the efficiency penalty peaks when trainees move into advanced skills, then simulation, deliberate practice, and competency checkpoints should be concentrated before and during that transition. The goal is not to reduce trainee exposure; it is to reduce low-value inefficiency once the trainee is in the procedure room.
Guest: Exactly. A structured curriculum could use simulation for scope control, lymph node station mapping, needle handling, bleeding control, and cough or sedation troubleshooting before trainees take on higher-complexity cases. Programs could also align case selection with trainee readiness: earlier rotations for basic airway navigation and sampling mechanics, later rotations for full procedural integration and independent decision-making under supervision.
Narrator: There are also implications for how institutions measure bronchoscopy suite productivity. If trainee-involved cases are judged against faculty-only efficiency expectations, training programs may look inefficient by design. The paper gives program leaders numbers to justify protected teaching time, appropriate scheduling templates, and realistic procedural throughput targets.
Guest: It also helps frame informed consent and patient communication in a professional way. Trainee participation is part of academic care, but the supervising structure must preserve patient-centered outcomes. In this study, with expert supervision, diagnostic yield and major safety were preserved. That is reassuring, but it does not remove the need for intentional oversight.
Narrator: The synthesis is that Chrissian and colleagues quantified what procedural educators already suspected: training has measurable costs in time and minor events, but those costs did not translate into worse diagnostic performance or more major complications in this setting.
Guest: The most useful takeaway is not that fellows should do fewer cases. It is that training should be engineered more deliberately. The steepest part of the learning curve deserves simulation, milestone-based progression, thoughtful case selection, and scheduling systems that recognize education as part of the procedure, not an inefficiency to be hidden.
Narrator: For the IP community, this paper provides evidence for a more honest conversation about advanced bronchoscopy training. The data support continued fellow participation with expert supervision, while reminding programs that procedural education consumes time, attention, and system capacity.
