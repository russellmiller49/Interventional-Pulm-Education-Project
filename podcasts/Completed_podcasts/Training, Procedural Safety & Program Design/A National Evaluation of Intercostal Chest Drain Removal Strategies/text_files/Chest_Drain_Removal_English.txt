The study question was straightforward: do intercostal chest drain removal strategies—particularly clamping, and also digital air leak devices—affect recurrence, repeat pleural intervention, or length of stay? The authors used data from the CLAMP project, a U K multicenter retrospective observational study, including patients admitted from May twenty twenty-one through October twenty twenty-three with primary or secondary spontaneous pneumothorax requiring an I C D.
Guest:  The inclusion criteria matter here. These were not patients with huge ongoing air leaks where nobody would reasonably clamp. The pneumothorax had persisted more than twenty-four hours, but had improved enough that drain removal was being considered. They excluded traumatic, iatrogenic, postsurgical, bilateral pneumothoraces, pleurodesis during the admission, accidental drain removal, death with the drain in situ, and some other groups. Clamping found something in some patients: twenty-four of two hundred fifty-eight clamping trials, or nine point three percent, identified pleural air reaccumulation. Among patients where clamping did not identify ongoing leak, thirty-five of two hundred thirty-four, or fifteen percent, still developed recurrent pneumothorax, and most of those required another intervention.
Guest:  Right, and that is where the “clamping as a safety test” idea starts to wobble. If a positive clamp identifies a subset with persistent air leak, that has value. But if a negative clamp still leaves a fifteen percent recurrence rate in that subgroup, then the negative predictive value is not what many clinicians probably hope it is. It may be testing for a pressure-dependent leak or a clinically meaningful leak over a short time window, but it does not reliably exclude a low-volume leak that declares itself later.

Guest:  That brings up confounding by indication. Clinicians may have clamped patients who seemed higher risk: slower resolution, more anxiety about recurrence, subtle radiographic concerns, or harder-to-characterize air leak. Guest:  Safety is also worth pausing on. Clamping-related adverse events occurred in two point three percent, or six patients. These included pain, breathlessness, subcutaneous emphysema, and one case consistent with tension pneumothorax. The authors estimate the rate of tension pneumothorax precipitated by clamping at zero point four percent.
Narrator: That is small, but not zero. And the nuance is that this may not mean clamping caused a complication that removal would have avoided. If the pleural defect was still open, removing the drain may have produced the same decompensation, but without the easy fix of unclamping. So clamping may occasionally convert a post-removal emergency into a pre-removal warning sign. That is clinically plausible, but this study cannot prove that.
Guest:  Now, digital air leak devices are interesting. Only fifty-eight patients had digital suction, and only twenty-four had the combination of digital air leak measurement plus clamping. Digital suction alone was not statistically associated with lower recurrence compared with wall suction or no suction. But the combination strategy had the lowest recurrence in the dataset: zero percent within seven days and four point two percent within thirty days, though the numbers were small and the P values were not significant.
Narrator: So that finding should be treated as hypothesis-generating. The “ultracautious” strategy may make sense in selected cases: high-risk S S P, marginal respiratory reserve, limited access to urgent reassessment, or a patient where recurrent pneumothorax would be especially consequential. But the dataset does not support a broad “digital plus clamp for everyone” conclusion.
Guest:  And there is another subtle issue: digital systems combine two things—suction and objective air leak measurement. In the setting of drain removal, the objective measurement may be the more important component, but the study cannot fully disentangle that. Also, thresholds for acceptable flow, duration of monitoring, timing of imaging, and local workflows were not standardized.
Narrator: Methodologically, the strengths are obvious: this is the largest dataset on I C D removal strategies that the authors identify, and it is multicenter, which matters because chest drain management is famously variable. The flowchart and recurrence curves make the clinical pathway very tangible.
Guest:  The limitations are equally important. It is retrospective. Removal strategy was not randomized. There was no prespecified primary hypothesis, so subgroup findings are exploratory. Patients undergoing pleurodesis were excluded, which may remove a higher-risk subset. Sex was not collected, limiting generalizability. And the study does not include patients with large persistent air leak, where clamping would generally be inappropriate anyway.
Narrator: So how should an I P audience use this? First, recurrent pneumothorax after apparent readiness for drain removal is not rare. A thirty-day recurrence rate around thirteen percent is enough to influence discharge counseling, safety-netting, follow-up imaging practices, and patient instructions.
Guest:  Second, routine clamping as a universal recurrence-prevention strategy is not supported here. It may detect some ongoing leaks, but it does not appear to reduce recurrence or repeat pleural intervention at the population level. The question becomes not “Should every drain be clamped?” but “Which patient, if any, benefits from that extra diagnostic step?”
Narrator: Third, workflow matters. A clamp trial is not free. It can prolong drain dwell time, delay discharge decisions, generate additional imaging, and create ambiguity when a small apical pneumothorax appears after clamping. And in a busy pleural service, those decisions compound.
Guest:  But the devil’s advocate position is fair: in an individual high-risk patient, clamping may still be reasonable if the goal is not to reduce recurrence across a population, but to avoid removing a functioning drain in someone who would decompensate quickly. The study pushes against routine clamping, not thoughtful selective clamping.
Narrator: The closing synthesis is this: Veale and colleagues provide a large, pragmatic look at real-world I C D removal practice in spontaneous pneumothorax. Recurrence after drain removal is common. Clamping appears generally safe but does not reduce recurrence or repeat pleural intervention in this retrospective dataset. Digital air leak measurement plus clamping is intriguing but underpowered. The next step is a prospective randomized trial that standardizes eligibility, clamp duration, imaging strategy, digital thresholds, and patient-centered outcomes.
Guest:  Until then, this paper supports a more explicit conversation around chest drain removal: risk of recurrence, rationale for or against clamping, local ability to monitor and reassess, and the patient’s physiology if recurrence occurs. In other words, less ritual, more risk stratification.
