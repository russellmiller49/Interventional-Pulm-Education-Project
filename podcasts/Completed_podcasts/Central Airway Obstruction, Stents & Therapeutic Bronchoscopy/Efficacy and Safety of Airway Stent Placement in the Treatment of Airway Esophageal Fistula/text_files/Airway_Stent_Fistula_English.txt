Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review, where we take one article and ask what it adds, what it does not prove, and how it should influence procedural thinking.

Guest: Today, we are discussing “Efficacy and Safety of Airway Stent Placement in the Treatment of Airway Esophageal Fistula,” by Xiangnan Li and colleagues, published in Respiration in 2025.

This is a single-center retrospective cohort comparing airway stent placement with conservative care in patients with esophageal cancer-associated airway-esophageal fistula, or A E F.

Narrator: This is a difficult clinical problem. Once an esophageal malignancy creates a fistula into the tracheobronchial tree, aspiration, recurrent infection, respiratory failure, and nutritional compromise can dominate the patient’s course.

Guest: Right. Many of these patients are not surgical candidates. Airway stenting is therefore a palliative mechanical intervention intended to seal the airway side of the defect, reduce contamination, relieve symptoms, and potentially create enough stability for nutrition or further cancer-directed therapy.

Narrator: Walk us through the study.

Guest: The investigators reviewed patients treated at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital from January 2017 through March 2024.

They included one hundred eleven adults with esophageal cancer-associated A E F. Fifty received an airway stent, and sixty-one received conservative care consisting of supportive treatment, antibiotics, and nutritional support.

Treatment was chosen according to clinical status and physician expertise, rather than random allocation. Survival from the date of fistula diagnosis was the primary endpoint. Complications and change in Karnofsky Performance Status, or K P S, were secondary endpoints.

Narrator: And the headline result is a fairly large survival difference.

Guest: It is. By the end of follow-up, ninety-eight of the one hundred eleven patients had died.

Mean survival was one hundred ninety-two point five days in the airway stent group. The ninety-five percent confidence interval ranged from one hundred fifty-one point two to two hundred thirty-three point seven days.

In the conservative-care group, mean survival was one hundred ten days. The confidence interval ranged from eighty-eight point one to one hundred thirty-one point eight days.

That is an absolute difference of eighty-two point five days, or almost twelve weeks. The P value was less than zero point zero zero one.

The Kaplan-Meier figure also reports a hazard ratio of two point one four, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval from one point four zero to three point two eight, in the direction favoring airway stenting.

One technical caveat is that the study reports mean survival in the presence of censoring but does not clearly define a restricted-mean time horizon. That makes the exact survival summary somewhat harder to interpret, even though the separation between the curves is visually substantial.

Narrator: Almost twelve additional weeks could be clinically meaningful in this population. But is this enough to conclude that stenting prolonged survival?

Guest: No. It is a strong association, not a causal estimate.

The central issue is confounding by indication and by treatment eligibility. Patients selected for stenting may have had technically favorable anatomy, enough physiologic reserve for bronchoscopy and anesthesia, a longer anticipated survival, or goals of care favoring intervention.

Patients managed conservatively may have been frailer, closer to death, or unable to tolerate a procedure.

The opposite bias is also possible, because more severe fistulas may preferentially prompt stenting. The direction and magnitude of the bias are therefore uncertain.

Narrator: The groups looked reasonably similar in the baseline table, apart from age.

Guest: They did on the measured variables. The stent group was younger, averaging sixty-five point five years versus seventy point one years. The authors report that age was not associated with survival in Cox regression, and they mention a matching analysis in the discussion.

But non-significant baseline P values do not establish comparability.

Unmeasured factors could include frailty, fistula geometry, degree of airway obstruction, aspiration severity, oxygen requirement, procedural tolerance, nutritional trajectory, available cancer-treatment options, and patient preference.

The matching analysis is not described in enough detail to resolve those concerns.

Narrator: The screening flow diagram raises another issue.

Guest: Yes. The investigators began with three hundred thirty-nine patients with malignant tracheoesophageal fistula. One hundred thirty-one were excluded for loss to follow-up. That is almost four in ten of the initially identified patients.

Another ninety-seven were excluded for reasons that included postoperative fistula, other tumor types, incomplete information, esophageal stenting, and combined airway and esophageal stenting.

That degree of attrition creates substantial potential for selection bias.

It also means the study does not answer the real-world strategy question of airway stenting versus esophageal stenting versus dual stenting. It compares one intervention with conservative management in a selected subset of the overall fistula population.

Narrator: There may also be a time-zero problem.

Guest: Exactly. Survival began at the date of A E F diagnosis.

If stent placement occurred later, and patients were classified as stented from the date of diagnosis, those patients had to survive long enough to receive the procedure. That could create immortal-time bias.

The diagnosis-to-stent interval is not clearly reported, and there is no time-dependent exposure analysis. That is another plausible mechanism by which the survival benefit could be overestimated.

Narrator: What about function and quality of life?

Guest: K P S increased from forty-eight point two before stenting to fifty-seven point nine afterward, with a P value of zero point zero one seven.

A roughly ten-point improvement may be clinically relevant. It suggests that some patients were more functional following the intervention.

But K P S is a clinician-rated functional-status measure, not a direct patient-reported quality-of-life instrument.

This was also a pre-post comparison within the stent group, without a clearly reported parallel change in the conservative group. The timing of reassessment is unclear, and patients who survived long enough to be reassessed may differ systematically from those who did not.

So this is a promising functional signal, but it is not definitive comparative evidence of improved quality of life.

Narrator: The authors describe airway stenting as safe, but the adverse-event rate was high.

Guest: Thirty-six of the fifty stented patients, or seventy-two percent, had a reported complication.

The authors list chest pain at fifty-two point eight percent, cough at twenty-seven point eight percent, and stent displacement at five point six percent. The denominator for those individual percentages is not fully transparent in the text.

Adverse events were identified retrospectively from clinical documentation. There was no standardized severity grading, and reporting of mucostasis, granulation tissue, recurrent aspiration, reintervention, and stent-related mortality was limited.

The study supports procedural feasibility. The safety conclusion should be more cautious.

Narrator: Did device type matter?

Guest: Thirty patients received a covered metallic stent, eighteen received a silicone stent, and two received a combination of devices that was excluded from the device comparison.

Survival did not differ by stent type, with a P value of zero point nine seven eight.

That is not evidence of equivalence. The groups were small, nonrandomized, and almost certainly selected according to anatomy and operator judgment. The analysis was not designed or powered to determine whether one device was superior.

Narrator: The paper also identifies several prognostic factors.

Guest: Male sex, T four disease, pulmonary infection, and larger fistula size were associated with shorter survival.

Prior surgery and higher baseline K P S were associated with longer survival.

Those findings are clinically plausible. However, the paper mainly provides P values rather than adjusted effect sizes and confidence intervals. That makes it difficult to assess the magnitude, precision, and stability of the associations.

They are best interpreted as exploratory prognostic signals.

Narrator: How should an interventional pulmonology team use this study?

Guest: As supportive evidence that airway stenting can be worthwhile in carefully selected patients, particularly when recurrent airway contamination is driving symptoms and the airway anatomy permits a durable seal.

Selection should account for fistula location and length, associated airway stenosis, distance from the carina and lobar orifices, prior radiation injury, secretion burden, active infection, and whether an esophageal stent is planned or already present.

The expected benefit must be weighed against anesthesia risk, migration, mucostasis, granulation, pressure injury, and the possibility that the stent will not completely seal an irregular defect.

Narrator: And the workflow is inherently multidisciplinary.

Guest: Absolutely. These cases often require coordination among interventional pulmonology, gastroenterology, thoracic surgery, oncology, nutrition, and palliative care.

The procedural plan should include objective confirmation that the fistula has been covered, an airway-clearance strategy, and surveillance for migration or obstruction.

The article does not provide enough technical detail on stent sizing, overlap margins, deployment, anesthesia, or post-placement testing to serve as a procedural guide. Its contribution is the clinical outcome signal rather than instruction on technique.

Narrator: What kind of study would move the field forward?

Guest: A prospective multicenter study with predefined treatment eligibility would help.

If randomization is not feasible, a target-trial approach could account for diagnosis-to-treatment timing, measured confounders, and changes in treatment over time.

Outcomes should include objective fistula sealing, aspiration events, oral intake, patient-reported symptom burden, hospital-free days, reintervention, standardized adverse-event grading, and survival.

Ideally, the study would compare airway stenting, esophageal stenting, dual stenting, and conservative care, with stratification by fistula anatomy and the presence or absence of clinically significant airway compromise.

Narrator: Give us the bottom line.

Guest: Li and colleagues found an eighty-two point five-day difference in mean survival favoring airway stenting, along with an improvement in K P S.

That is an important and clinically plausible signal in a population with very limited options.

But this remains a selected, single-center retrospective cohort with major loss to follow-up, possible immortal-time bias, unmeasured confounding, incomplete comparison of stent strategies, and limited safety characterization.

The study supports offering airway stenting to appropriate patients. It does not prove that the stent itself caused the survival difference, and it does not justify universal stenting.

Narrator: That is the key takeaway. Airway stenting may provide meaningful palliation and possibly longer survival, but the decision still rests on anatomy, procedural fitness, goals of care, and multidisciplinary judgment.
