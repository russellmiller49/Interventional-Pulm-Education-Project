Narrator: Welcome to Interventional Pulm dot com, I P Journal Review. Today we’re discussing Wayne and colleagues, published in Annals of the American Thoracic Society in 2026, titled “Temporal Trends and Mortality in Tracheobronchial Stenting in the United States: a national cohort study.”
Guest: And this is a paper that lands right in the center of interventional pulmonology practice. Airway stenting is one of those interventions that can feel dramatically effective in the right patient: immediate airway patency, relief of obstruction, a bridge to systemic therapy or radiation, or palliation of severe dyspnea. But it is also a procedure with meaningful downstream consequences ... mucus plugging, migration, granulation tissue, repeat bronchoscopies, and the basic question of whether the patient’s trajectory is actually modifiable.
Narrator: Exactly. The paper asks a deceptively simple epidemiologic question: how often are tracheobronchial stents being placed in the United States, where are they being placed ... inpatient versus ambulatory ... and what are the observed inpatient outcomes? The authors use the National Inpatient Sample from 2016 through 2022, and then separately use State Inpatient Databases and State Ambulatory Surgery and Services Databases from six states in 2019 to estimate the inpatient-versus-ambulatory split.
Guest: The scale is the strength here. This is not a single-center airway program reporting its outcomes. The N I S represents more than 97 percent of acute care hospitalizations in the U.S. after weighting, so this gives us a population-level view of inpatient airway stenting. Of course, that comes at the cost of granularity. We get coding-based procedure identification, not bronchoscopic anatomy, not stent type, not exact indication, and not symptom response.
Narrator: Right. So let’s start with the headline numbers. Across U.S. hospitalizations from 2016 to 2022, the incidence of inpatient tracheobronchial stenting was 0.71 hospitalizations per 100,000 residents, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 0.66 to 0.76. The rate increased from 0.58 per 100,000 in 2016 to 0.67 in 2022, peaking at 0.77 in 2021.
Guest: That is a 16 percent increase over the study period. It is statistically significant, but the authors are appropriately cautious that with this many hospitalizations, statistical significance is not the same as clinical significance. Still, the direction is interesting, especially because one might have expected earlier lung cancer detection, better systemic therapy, and stage migration to reduce some malignant central airway obstruction burden.
Narrator: And yet the malignant component increased. Among hospitalized patients receiving tracheobronchial stents, 69.7 percent had a diagnosis of malignancy, 59.1 percent had lung cancer, and 40.7 percent had metastatic cancer. The malignancy proportion rose from 65.9 percent in 2016 to 74.6 percent in 2022.
Guest: That is one of the more interesting signals in the paper. It suggests that inpatient stenting is still heavily tied to malignant disease, and perhaps increasingly so. But the tricky part is that administrative data cannot tell us whether these were emergent obstructions, planned inpatient procedures, malignant extrinsic compression, mixed disease, fistula management, post-treatment airway changes, or even benign disease coded alongside cancer.
Narrator: The mortality signal is what will get everyone’s attention. Overall inpatient mortality among patients receiving tracheobronchial stents was 12.3 percent. In-hospital mortality rose from 11.3 percent in 2016 to 12.7 percent in 2022. In patients with cancer, mortality was higher ... 13.7 percent compared with 9.0 percent in those without cancer.
Guest: And that has to be interpreted carefully. This is not procedural mortality. The database cannot establish that the stent caused death, contributed to death, or failed to prevent death. But it does establish something clinically important: the hospitalized population receiving airway stents is high risk. Nearly one in eight died before discharge. That matters for consent, goals-of-care framing, multidisciplinary planning, and selection.
Narrator: The timing analysis is also provocative. About 52.9 percent underwent early stenting, defined as within three days of hospitalization, and 47.1 percent underwent late stenting. Mortality was 8.9 percent when stenting was early versus 16.1 percent when stenting was late. Mean length of stay was 7 days in the early group versus 19 days in the late group.
Guest: That is tempting to read as “earlier is better,” but that would be too causal. Late stenting may simply identify patients who are sicker, deteriorating, harder to optimize, delayed in diagnosis, delayed in transfer, or only recognized as stent candidates after other treatments fail. The paper notes that patients who died were older, had more comorbidities, more acute organ failures, and more often underwent late stenting.
Narrator: Still, for an I P service, this does raise a workflow question. If central airway obstruction is suspected, does delayed I P involvement lead to a worse procedural window? Are patients spending several days cycling through noninvasive ventilation, steroids, antibiotics, or oncologic workup before anyone asks whether there is a mechanical airway problem that could be palliated?
Guest: That’s the key clinical translation. The study does not prove that early intervention reduces mortality, but it supports a systems-level hypothesis: early recognition and triage of central airway obstruction may matter. For example, earlier review of C T imaging, bronchoscopy candidacy, airway anesthesia risk, availability of rigid bronchoscopy, and oncology/radiation coordination could plausibly change who reaches the procedure suite in a salvage scenario versus a planned scenario.
Narrator: The inpatient-versus-ambulatory analysis is also very relevant. Across six states in 2019 ... Florida, Iowa, Maryland, Nebraska, New York, and Wisconsin ... 73.3 percent of tracheobronchial stenting procedures were performed during inpatient hospitalization, while 26.7 percent were ambulatory.
Guest: And outcomes were dramatically different by setting. In the state-level analysis, inpatients were older and much sicker: 23.8 percent received invasive mechanical ventilation and 33.5 percent had acute hypoxic respiratory failure. Inpatient mortality was 11.8 percent, while ambulatory mortality was 0 percent, and 96.6 percent of ambulatory patients were discharged home.
Narrator: Which is reassuring for appropriately selected ambulatory patients, but it also tells us we should not quote outpatient stenting safety data when counseling a hospitalized patient on pressors, ventilated, with metastatic cancer and lobar collapse. Those are different populations.
Guest: Exactly. The mortality comparison is not an argument that ambulatory stenting is inherently safer because the procedural technique is different. It reflects selection. Ambulatory patients are well enough to arrive for an outpatient procedure and go home. Inpatients are often in physiologic extremis or close to it. But that difference is precisely why setting-specific outcome data are useful.
Narrator: Regional variation is another major finding. In 2022, inpatient stenting incidence ranged from 1.32 per 100,000 residents in the East North Central Division ... Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Ohio, and Wisconsin ... to 0.26 per 100,000 in the Mountain Division.
Guest: That is a fivefold regional spread. At the state level, combined inpatient and ambulatory incidence in the six-state 2019 analysis ranged from 0.57 to 1.60 per 100,000. The proportion performed inpatient ranged from 52.6 percent to 92.6 percent. So we are not just seeing different volumes; we are seeing different site-of-care patterns.
Narrator: The obvious question is whether this reflects disease burden, referral pathways, I P workforce distribution, hospital coding, stent availability, payer effects, or true differences in practice philosophy.
Guest: Probably all of the above. Regions with more I P training programs or high-volume airway centers may have more access. But access cuts both ways. More access may mean appropriate palliation for patients who otherwise would not have options. It may also mean lower thresholds for placing stents in borderline cases where benefit is uncertain. Administrative epidemiology cannot adjudicate appropriateness.
Narrator: Methodologically, the study is clean for what it is, but the limitations are central. Identification of stenting depends on I C D 10 procedural codes for inpatient cases and C P T codes for ambulatory cases. Prior work supports high specificity for procedure codes, but misclassification is still possible.
Guest: And the bigger issue is missing clinical detail. We do not know whether obstruction was intrinsic, extrinsic, or mixed. We do not know whether the stent was silicone, fully covered metal, uncovered metal, hybrid, Y-stent, tracheal, bronchial, or carinal. We do not know whether rigid bronchoscopy was used, whether tumor debulking or thermal therapy preceded stent placement, whether the patient had fistula, malacia, transplant airway complication, post-intubation stenosis, or malignant C A O.
Narrator: We also do not know symptom response, dyspnea score, airway diameter restoration, technical success, stent-related adverse events, reintervention rates, discharge to cancer therapy, or patient-centered outcomes. That is a major limitation because airway stenting is often palliative. A patient may die during the same hospitalization but still have had meaningful relief of distress, or the procedure may have been futile. This database cannot tell us which occurred.
Guest: That distinction is critical. Mortality alone is a blunt instrument in palliative airway intervention. The right outcome might be extubation, ability to lie flat for radiation, discharge home, relief of suffocation, time to systemic therapy, avoidance of emergent death, or simply a patient-defined goal. But at the population level, 12 percent in-hospital mortality is still too large to ignore.
Narrator: How should this affect practice? Not as a reason to stop stenting. The paper does not say stents are unsafe or ineffective. It says the real-world inpatient population receiving stents is high risk, and current practice varies substantially.
Guest: It should probably sharpen the pre-procedure conversation. For hospitalized C A O patients, especially those with malignancy, acute organ dysfunction, mechanical ventilation, or delayed stenting, the consent discussion should include the possibility that stenting may improve airway patency without altering the overall hospitalization outcome. That is not nihilism; it is honest procedural medicine.
Narrator: It also supports better prospective data capture. The field needs registries that include indication, obstruction type, stent type, procedural modality, anesthesia approach, technical success, symptom improvement, quality of life, reintervention, complications, and post-procedure trajectory. Ideally, we would distinguish a patient who benefits and dies later of progressive cancer from a patient who undergoes a technically successful but clinically nonbeneficial intervention.
Guest: And we need to define the denominator better. How many patients with C A O are evaluated but not stented? How many are treated with debulking alone, radiation alone, systemic therapy, surgical resection, tracheostomy, or comfort-focused care? Without that, procedure incidence tells us utilization, but not appropriateness.
Narrator: The authors place the findings in the context of recent guidelines suggesting tracheobronchial stenting for palliation of symptomatic central airway obstruction not amenable to definitive surgical intervention. The practical point is that “suggesting” stenting is not the same as recommending stenting for every obstructed airway. Patient selection is the whole game.
Guest: Right. Stents are tools, not destinations. They are most compelling when there is extrinsic compression, mixed obstruction after debulking, airway malacia, fistula management, or a need to maintain patency for a defined downstream goal. They are less compelling when obstruction is purely endoluminal and can be debulked without residual instability, or when the patient’s overall condition makes any durable benefit unlikely.
Narrator: The paper also indirectly reminds us that the procedure does not end when the stent is deployed. Follow-up bronchoscopy, secretion management, infection risk, migration, granulation tissue, and repeat interventions are part of the therapeutic package. For an inpatient with limited prognosis, those downstream burdens should be weighed against the expected benefit.
Guest: And for systems, the regional variation raises uncomfortable but important questions. Are some areas underusing stents because patients lack access to I P? Are others overusing stents because the technology is available and deployment is easier? Are transfers happening too late? Are patients being referred before oncology and radiation options are clarified? This study cannot answer those questions, but it makes them hard to avoid.
Narrator: So, final take-home. Wayne and colleagues provide a national view of tracheobronchial stenting in the United States. Inpatient stenting is uncommon but increased modestly from 2016 to 2022. Most hospitalized patients receiving stents had malignancy. Most stenting, at least in the six-state analysis, occurred during inpatient hospitalization. And among inpatients, mortality before discharge was high ... about one in eight.
Guest: The most useful way to apply this paper is not to conclude that airway stenting is good or bad. It is to recognize that inpatient airway stenting occurs in a very high-risk population, with substantial regional and site-of-care variation, and that the field needs better prospective, patient-centered outcome data. For now, it should make I P clinicians more deliberate about timing, selection, consent, and defining what success actually means before entering the airway.
Narrator: Well said. That wraps this episode of Interventional Pulm dot com, I P Journal Review. The article adds important epidemiologic context to a procedure that many of us think about technically, but perhaps not always at the population level. The next step is moving from “how often are we placing stents?” to “which patients are truly helped, under what circumstances, and at what cost?” Thanks for listening.
