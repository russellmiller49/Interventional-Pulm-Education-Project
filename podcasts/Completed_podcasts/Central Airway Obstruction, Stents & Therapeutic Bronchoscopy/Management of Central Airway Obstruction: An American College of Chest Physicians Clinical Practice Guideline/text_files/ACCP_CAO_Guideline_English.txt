Narrator: Welcome to the Interventional Pulm dot com I.P. Journal Review. Today we’re discussing Mahmood and colleagues’ American College of Chest Physicians clinical practice guideline on the management of central airway obstruction, published in Chest in twenty twenty-five.
Guest: And this is a big one for interventional pulmonology. Central airway obstruction is one of those syndromes where everyone agrees it matters, but the evidence base is surprisingly thin once you start asking specific comparative questions: rigid versus flexible, general anesthesia versus moderate sedation, stent versus no stent, surveillance bronchoscopy versus symptom-driven follow-up, surgery versus bronchoscopic management.
Narrator: Exactly. The guideline defines C.A.O. as at least fifty percent occlusion of the trachea, mainstem bronchi, bronchus intermedius, or lobar bronchi. It includes malignant and nonmalignant etiologies, and anatomically the paper uses the familiar intrinsic, extrinsic, and mixed framework. That distinction is not just descriptive. It determines whether the dominant strategy is debulking, dilation, stenting, systemic therapy, radiation, surgery, or some combination.
Guest: The visual algorithm in the paper is useful there. On page five, Figure two separates malignant from nonmalignant C.A.O. For malignant C.A.O., the pathway emphasizes surgical candidacy, debridement or ablation for endobronchial tumor, systemic chemo-immunotherapy and radiation, and stent placement when extrinsic compression or tumor progression prevents durable patency. For nonmalignant C.A.O., the algorithm shifts toward systemic medical therapy when relevant, dilation with or without excision or ablation, local bronchoscopic therapy for recurrence, and stenting further downstream.
Narrator: Methodologically, this was a multidisciplinary Chest panel using PEE-ko questions, systematic literature review, Grade methodology, and a modified Delphi process. They screened nine thousand, six hundred eighty-eight abstracts, reviewed one hundred fifty full-text articles, and included thirty-one studies. That sounds like a large effort, but the punchline is that the overall certainty of evidence was very low.
Guest: That’s the key tension in this guideline. The recommendations are clinically recognizable and probably align with how many experienced airway programs already think, but almost all of them are conditional and based on very low certainty evidence. So this is not a document saying, “The data definitively prove the best approach.” It is more, “Given the limited comparative evidence and the realities of life-threatening airway disease, here is the most defensible framework.”
Narrator: Let’s walk through the major recommendations. The first is a good practice statement: patients with suspected C.A.O. should undergo a comprehensive history and physical exam focused on the respiratory system, C.T. imaging of the chest, and appropriate labs, especially when nonmalignant etiologies or preoperative concerns are on the table.
Guest: That seems obvious until it isn’t. These patients get mislabeled as C.O.P.D. exacerbations, asthma, pneumonia, anxiety, or progression of underlying malignancy. The guideline specifically notes the importance of C.T. chest and neck for diagnosis, severity assessment, procedural planning, and follow-up. It also emphasizes airway assessment details that matter for rigid bronchoscopy and intubation: mouth opening, dentition, Mallampati, neck mobility, and thyromental distance.
Narrator: The second recommendation is probably the core I.P. message: for symptomatic malignant or nonmalignant C.A.O., the panel suggests therapeutic bronchoscopy as an adjunct to systemic therapy and, or, local radiation. Conditional recommendation, very low certainty.
Guest: And the word “adjunct” matters. In malignant C.A.O., bronchoscopy often buys time, relieves obstruction, reverses atelectasis, improves functional status, and allows systemic or radiation therapy to actually happen. In nonmalignant C.A.O., bronchoscopy may be paired with treatment of vasculitis, infection, inflammatory disease, or post-transplant airway complications.
Narrator: The guideline cites limited comparative data. One prospective study compared thirty-four N.S.C.L.C. patients who underwent therapeutic bronchoscopy with twelve who declined it. Both groups received chemoradiation. Mean survival was ten, plus or minus nine, months with bronchoscopy versus four, plus or minus three, months without, with a log-rank P-value of point zero zero five. Dyspnea decreased, and global health and physical function improved for up to six months.
Guest: There’s also the retrospective Evermore-type signal: patients with lung cancer-associated C.A.O. receiving therapeutic bronchoscopy plus chemoradiation had better survival than those receiving chemoradiation alone, with a reported hazard ratio of two point one and a ninety-five percent confidence interval from one point one to four point eight. But we have to be careful. These are not clean causal estimates. Selection bias is unavoidable. Patients who survive to bronchoscopy, tolerate bronchoscopy, or are referred to bronchoscopy are probably different from those who are not.
Narrator: Right. The practical interpretation is not that every patient with malignant C.A.O. must undergo bronchoscopy. It is that symptomatic C.A.O. should trigger urgent consideration of therapeutic bronchoscopy, especially when airway patency is the limiting factor for definitive or palliative oncologic therapy.
Guest: Then recommendation three: rigid bronchoscopy is suggested over flexible bronchoscopy for therapeutic interventions. Again, conditional, very low certainty.
Narrator: The Aquire registry is important here. It included nine hundred forty-seven patients undergoing three hundred eighty-two flexible and seven hundred thirty-three rigid therapeutic bronchoscopies. Technical success, defined as reopening the airway lumen to greater than fifty percent of normal diameter, was very similar: ninety-two point seven percent with flexible bronchoscopy versus ninety-three point five percent with rigid bronchoscopy, P equals point six two.
Guest: So the recommendation is not driven by a dramatic numerical superiority in technical success. It’s driven by procedural logic and expert consensus. Rigid bronchoscopy provides ventilation, airway control, tamponade capacity, large-bore suction, the ability to core through tumor, and a platform for silicone stent placement. In a proximal, critical, bleeding, mixed-obstruction airway, those features matter.
Narrator: The devil’s advocate question is whether the recommendation could be overgeneralized. For distal lobar obstruction, noncritical lesions, selected endobronchial disease, or centers with specific flexible expertise, flexible therapeutic bronchoscopy may be reasonable. But for high-risk proximal C.A.O., the guideline clearly favors having rigid capability.
Guest: And that has system-level implications. If a hospital is managing malignant airway obstruction but lacks rigid bronchoscopy, anesthesia partnership, stent inventory, and personnel comfortable with airway rescue, this guideline implicitly supports building referral pathways rather than improvising definitive airway therapy in marginal settings.
Narrator: Recommendation four addresses sedation: general anesthesia or deep sedation is suggested over moderate sedation for therapeutic bronchoscopy. The Aquire data again show no difference in technical success, but general anesthesia or deep sedation was associated with fewer complications compared with moderate sedation, with a multivariable odds ratio of point four two, ninety-five percent confidence interval point two one to point eight three, P equals point zero one three.
Guest: And paralytics were associated with fewer complications as well: three percent versus six point seven percent, P equals point zero zero six. That is very relevant because there has always been a philosophical tension between preserving spontaneous ventilation and achieving procedural control. In experienced hands, with the right team, controlled ventilation and paralysis may reduce coughing, movement, hypoxemic episodes, and procedural chaos.
Narrator: But this is also where individual anatomy and physiology matter. A patient with near-complete distal tracheal obstruction, severe dynamic collapse, or bilateral mainstem compromise is not the same as a stable patient with a short, benign stenosis. The guideline appropriately emphasizes multidisciplinary planning with anesthesia.
Guest: Recommendation five is refreshingly non-dogmatic: for rigid therapeutic bronchoscopy under general anesthesia, either jet ventilation or controlled or spontaneous assisted ventilation can be used. In Aquire, controlled or spontaneous assisted volume-cycled ventilation was used in seven hundred fourteen patients, and jet ventilation in two hundred thirty. Technical success was ninety-two point nine percent versus ninety-six point one percent, respectively, without significant differences in dyspnea, quality of life, complications, or complication-related mortality.
Narrator: So this is not a “jet ventilation is mandatory” guideline. It is more about matching ventilation strategy to lesion anatomy, equipment, anesthesiology expertise, and rescue planning. They also mention EK-mo for critical tracheal or bilateral mainstem obstruction, but appropriately frame ideal patient selection and complication profiles as poorly understood.
Guest: Recommendation six: for symptomatic C.A.O. with endobronchial disease, the panel suggests tumor or tissue excision and, or, ablation to achieve airway patency. That’s intuitive, but the guideline is honest that no comparative malignant tumor excision studies were identified. The support comes from lower-quality comparative data in nonmalignant disease and noncomparative case series in malignant obstruction.
Narrator: The guideline lists familiar modalities: forceps debridement, rigid coring, microdebridement, cryodebridement, laser, electrocautery, and argon plasma coagulation. It also makes the safety point that when using heat-based therapies, F.I.O.2. should be reduced to point four or less, and ignition risk around flammable devices should be minimized.
Guest: That point is basic but non-negotiable. Airway fire is rare, catastrophic, and preventable enough that it should be built into every procedural time-out involving thermal therapy.
Narrator: Recommendation seven focuses on nonmalignant stenosis: airway dilation is suggested either alone or combined with other modalities. The interesting data come from idiopathic subglottic stenosis. In a prospective multicenter study of eight hundred ten patients, recurrence at three years was twenty-eight percent with dilation and twelve point four percent with endoscopic carbon dioxide laser resection plus adjuvant medical therapy. At five years, recurrence was fifty percent with dilation and thirty percent with that endoscopic resection-medical approach.
Guest: Which complicates the idea of dilation as “first-line.” It may be first-line because it is accessible, repeatable, and often effective, but recurrence is common. The real question is phenotype: simple web-like stenosis versus complex cartilage injury, idiopathic subglottic stenosis versus post-intubation stenosis, inflammatory disease versus transplant anastomotic stenosis. A single recommendation can’t fully capture that heterogeneity.
Narrator: Recommendation eight is stenting: for symptomatic malignant or nonmalignant C.A.O., stent placement is suggested if other therapeutic bronchoscopic and systemic treatments have failed and when feasible for the underlying disorder.
Guest: I like that wording because it resists the temptation to stent every airway that looks scary. Stents are powerful, but they are not benign. Mucus plugging, granulation tissue, migration, fracture, and infection are not theoretical complications. The guideline essentially says: use stents when airway patency cannot otherwise be maintained, especially with extrinsic compression or tumor progression, but avoid them when debridement alone gives a durable airway.
Narrator: The Spock trial is notable. Seventy-eight patients with malignant endobronchial C.A.O. due to N.S.C.L.C. were randomized to silicone stent or no stent after tumor debridement. There was no difference in recurrence-free or overall survival, but stenting produced more durable dyspnea improvement and reduced the need for repeat therapeutic bronchoscopies. The subset signal was strongest in patients who had failed first-line chemotherapy or were receiving radiation or palliation only, with a hazard ratio for local recurrence of point two one, ninety-five percent confidence interval point zero six to point seven four.
Guest: And in Aquire, four hundred six stents were placed during one thousand, one hundred fifteen therapeutic bronchoscopies. Stent placement was associated with technical success, with an odds ratio of eleven point nine, but not with improvement in dyspnea, quality of life, or complications. That’s a useful reminder that a technically successful airway is not always a patient-centered success.
Narrator: For benign disease, the guideline remains cautious, especially because of the F.D.A. warning about metallic stents in benign tracheal disorders. New-generation fully covered metallic stents may be safer, but the evidence is still limited. In benign disease, stenting should usually feel like a trial, a bridge, or a fallback after repeated failure—not a casual default.
Guest: Recommendation nine is intentionally permissive: after stent placement, either routine surveillance bronchoscopy or symptom-driven bronchoscopy is suggested. The evidence was basically one retrospective study. In that study, routine surveillance within three months found stent-related complications in sixteen of thirty-nine patients, but most were symptomatic anyway. In the symptom-driven group, twenty-six of thirty-one symptomatic patients had complications detected.
Narrator: So the guideline does not mandate routine surveillance bronchoscopy. It suggests clinical follow-up, airway clearance strategies such as hypertonic saline nebulization, and C.T. assessment when appropriate. The practical choice depends on stent type, indication, patient reliability, secretion burden, prior mucus plugging, oncologic trajectory, and access to urgent bronchoscopy.
Guest: Recommendation ten addresses local bronchoscopic therapies—P.D.T., brachytherapy, cryotherapy, intralesional chemotherapy, steroid, mitomycin C—and says either using or holding these therapies is reasonable. That may sound like a non-recommendation, but it reflects the evidence. The data are too heterogeneous and biased for a strong directive.
Narrator: In malignant disease, one retrospective comparison found mean time to reintervention of one hundred forty-seven days with P.D.T. versus ninety-eight days with non-P.D.T. ablation plus chemoradiation, but P equals point two zero. In benign tracheal stenosis, a small retrospective study reported four-month success rates of fifteen percent for dilation, eighteen point two percent for steroid injection, and seventy-five percent for mitomycin C application. Interesting, but not definitive.
Guest: Finally, surgery. For nonmalignant C.A.O., the panel suggests either open surgical resection or therapeutic bronchoscopy. For malignant C.A.O. with endobronchial tumor, either surgical resection or therapeutic bronchoscopy can be used for initial obstruction relief, with the important caveat that surgery has the clearest role in localized disease and carcinoid-type tumors.
Narrator: The idiopathic subglottic stenosis data are again instructive. At three years, recurrence was twenty-eight percent with dilation, twelve point four percent with endoscopic laser resection plus adjuvant medical therapy, and one point two percent with surgical resection. At five years, recurrence was fifty percent, thirty percent, and five percent, respectively. But surgery came with worse voice outcomes compared with other therapies.
Guest: That is the ideal example of why patient-centered outcomes matter. The “best airway” is not automatically the best outcome if voice, swallowing, perioperative risk, or patient preference is unacceptable. For complex recurrent stenosis, surgery may be the most durable option. For simple stenosis, high-risk surgical candidates, or patients prioritizing voice preservation, bronchoscopic management may be entirely appropriate.
Narrator: So, what does this guideline add for an I.P. audience?
Guest: It codifies a practical airway philosophy: evaluate carefully, image well, involve the right team early, use therapeutic bronchoscopy as an adjunct rather than an isolated event, favor rigid bronchoscopy and deeper anesthesia for high-risk therapeutic work, reserve stents for situations where patency cannot otherwise be maintained, and keep surgery in the conversation for selected benign stenosis and localized malignant disease.
Narrator: And what remains uncertain is almost everything comparative. Which ablative modality is best? Who truly needs a stent after debulking? What is the optimal surveillance strategy? Which benign stenosis phenotype should go earlier to surgery? What ventilation strategy is safest for which anatomy? How do we measure success beyond lumen diameter?
Guest: That may be the most important critique. The field needs prospective, patient-centered, phenotype-specific studies. Technical success defined as getting the airway above fifty percent of normal diameter is useful, but patients care about dyspnea, voice, secretion burden, hospital days, ability to receive cancer therapy, tracheostomy avoidance, durability, and quality of life.
Narrator: The bottom line: this Chest guideline is not a high-certainty evidence map. It is a consensus-guided framework built on limited data for a high-stakes clinical syndrome. For interventional pulmonologists, it supports what many airway programs already believe: central airway obstruction should be managed deliberately, with multimodality capability, anesthesia partnership, multidisciplinary coordination, and careful restraint around stents and other durable implants.
Guest: And perhaps the most honest message is that central airway obstruction is not one disease. Malignant extrinsic compression, friable endobronchial tumor, post-intubation tracheal stenosis, idiopathic subglottic stenosis, transplant anastomotic stenosis, and tracheobronchomalacia all live under the C.A.O. umbrella, but they should not be treated as interchangeable.
