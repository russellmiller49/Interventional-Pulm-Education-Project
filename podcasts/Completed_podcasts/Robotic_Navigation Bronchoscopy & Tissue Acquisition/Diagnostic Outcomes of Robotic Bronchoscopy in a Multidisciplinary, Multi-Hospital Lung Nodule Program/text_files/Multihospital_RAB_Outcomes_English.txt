Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. Today we’re looking at a robotic bronchoscopy outcomes paper from Deitz and colleagues, published in the Journal of Surgical Research in 2026. The article is titled, “Diagnostic Outcomes of Robotic Bronchoscopy in a Multidisciplinary, Multi-Hospital Lung Nodule Program.”
Guest: And this is a very relevant paper for interventional pulmonologists because it asks a practical question: what does robotic bronchoscopy look like when it moves beyond a single expert operator or a highly controlled trial environment, and into a multi-hospital, multi-operator lung nodule program?
Narrator: Exactly. This study evaluated robotic bronchoscopy using the Monarch platform across four hospitals in a single academic health system. The cases occurred from August 2018 through April 2023. The authors included patients with suspicious intraparenchymal lung lesions biopsied by either pulmonologists or thoracic surgeons.
Guest: The scale is the first thing that stands out. They report one thousand twenty-two procedural encounters and one thousand eighty-four lesions biopsied, performed by sixteen operators: seven pulmonologists and nine thoracic surgeons. That gives the paper a different flavor from many early robotic bronchoscopy series, where the results may reflect a small number of highly invested proceduralists.
Narrator: Right. And the workflow is also worth noting. All cases used the Monarch robotic bronchoscope under general anesthesia with orotracheal intubation. Two-dimensional fluoroscopy was used in nearly all cases, about 96.6 percent. Traditional linear EBUS for lymph node evaluation was performed in 53.5 percent. Radial EBUS use was at the discretion of the operator. Importantly, they did not use cone-beam CT.
Guest: That matters. A lot of current discussions around robotic bronchoscopy are really discussions about robotic bronchoscopy plus advanced intraprocedural imaging. This paper is more about robotic bronchoscopy in a relatively pragmatic workflow: robotic navigation, fluoroscopy, selective radial EBUS, and no cone-beam CT.
Narrator: The lesions were mostly the kind of nodules many IP programs see every week. Median long-axis size was 25 millimeters, most were solid, about 84 percent, and about 62 percent were peripheral. A bronchus sign was present in about 70 percent. Median imaging follow-up was just over one year.
Guest: The follow-up is a major strength. The authors were not just reporting immediate procedural yield. They assessed diagnostic accuracy against final known pathology and longitudinal imaging. Benign diagnoses required either confirmatory biopsy or radiographic resolution or stability, with additional analysis using one-year follow-up. That approach is more clinically meaningful than a same-day “diagnostic versus nondiagnostic” label, but it also introduces the usual follow-up and verification issues.
Narrator: For the primary diagnostic accuracy analysis, 865 lesions were included. The overall diagnostic accuracy was 82.7 percent, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 80.1 to 85.2 percent.
Guest: That is a solid number. It sits in the range many clinicians would consider acceptable for a bronchoscopic approach, especially when the chest tube rate is low. But it is important not to confuse diagnostic accuracy with sensitivity for malignancy. The sensitivity for malignancy was reported at 71.8 percent. The robotic bronchoscope made a malignant diagnosis in 470 lesions, but 185 malignant nodules were not diagnosed by robotic bronchoscopy and required another diagnostic route, such as repeat robotic bronchoscopy, CT-guided FNA, or surgical resection.
Narrator: That distinction is key. The headline diagnostic accuracy is 82.7 percent, but if the clinical concern is, “How reassured should I be by a benign or atypical bronchoscopic result in a high-risk nodule?” the answer is more nuanced.
Guest: Absolutely. And the indeterminate group reinforces that point. Atypia of uncertain significance occurred in 102 cases, or 9.4 percent. Of those indeterminate lesions, 58.8 percent went on to resection or rebiopsy, and malignancy was found in 81.7 percent of those cases. Another eleven patients were referred for empiric SBRT because of high suspicion.
Narrator: So atypia in this cohort was not a soft benign result.
Guest: No. For IP clinicians, that is probably one of the most important practical takeaways. An atypical robotic bronchoscopy result, especially in a high-risk lesion, needs a structured follow-up pathway. That may mean repeat biopsy with better imaging confirmation, CT-guided biopsy, surgical diagnosis, or multidisciplinary treatment planning. But it should not be treated as reassuring by default.
Narrator: Let’s talk about predictors of diagnostic success. In univariate analysis, larger lesion size, bronchus sign, intraoperative direct endobronchial visualization, and radial EBUS localization were associated with successful diagnosis. The overall yield for lesions with a bronchus sign was 85.5 percent.
Guest: The size gradient is also clinically useful. For lesions between 1 and 2 centimeters, diagnostic accuracy was 74 percent. For lesions larger than 2 centimeters, the combined diagnostic accuracy was 86.3 percent. The highest rate was in solid lesions larger than 3 centimeters, where accuracy reached 89 percent.
Narrator: And the small subsolid lesions were more challenging.
Guest: Yes. Small ground-glass or mixed-density lesions under 2 centimeters had lower diagnostic success, around 70 percent, though the numbers in those subgroups were small. That is exactly where many programs are debating whether to add cone-beam CT, digital tomosynthesis, augmented fluoroscopy, needle-based localization, or go directly to surgical biopsy when appropriate.
Narrator: The multivariable analysis identified bronchus sign and radial EBUS localization as favorable predictors. Higher body mass index, repeat Monarch procedure, and right-sided lesions were associated with lower likelihood of success. The reported odds ratios were modest, but the directionality is interesting.
Guest: The right-sided finding is particularly intriguing because the paper also highlights the right upper lobe as a recurring problem area. Right upper lobe lesions represented a substantial proportion of unreachable lesions and indeterminate results, especially the apical segment. That tracks with what many proceduralists worry about anatomically: angulation, catheter stability, tool trajectory, and divergence between the planned pathway and the actual biopsy vector.
Narrator: There is also a detail in the paper around central versus peripheral location that deserves careful reading. The authors report peripheral location as associated with successful diagnosis, but the table presentation makes the directionality somewhat tricky because it reports distributions within success and failure groups rather than simple success rates by region.
Guest: Right. That is a good reminder that when reading these procedural papers, the cross-tab percentages can be misleading if you do not pause and ask, “What is the denominator?” The more reproducible predictors here are probably size, bronchus sign, and confirmation of tool-in-lesion or at least lesion localization with radial EBUS.
Narrator: Now complications. Pneumothorax occurred in 51 patients, or 5 percent. Tube thoracostomy was required in 33 cases, which was 3 percent of all cases. Postoperative respiratory failure occurred in 0.8 percent, a new supplemental oxygen requirement in 0.7 percent, and three patients, or 0.3 percent, remained intubated postoperatively for endobronchial bleeding, all extubated the next day after toilet bronchoscopy.
Guest: Those complication rates are favorable compared with historical CT-guided transthoracic biopsy data, where pneumothorax is a major tradeoff. The paper frames CT-guided FNA as highly accurate, approaching the mid-90 percent range in some series, but with pneumothorax rates that can be substantial. So the clinical question becomes: how much diagnostic certainty are we willing to trade for a lower pleural complication rate?
Narrator: And that tradeoff is lesion-specific.
Guest: Exactly. A 3-centimeter solid lesion with a bronchus sign and radial EBUS confirmation is a very different case from a 9-millimeter ground-glass lesion in the right upper lobe apex. Lumping those into one robotic bronchoscopy performance estimate may be convenient, but it does not help much with actual procedural planning.
Narrator: The learning curve analysis is also interesting. Operator success improved over the first 130 cases, from a baseline around 72 percent to a plateau around 84 percent. Procedure time decreased with experience, but only by about four minutes over the first 100 cases.
Guest: That suggests two things. First, the technology appears adoptable across different operators and specialties without catastrophic early performance. Second, there is still a meaningful learning curve for accuracy. The time savings may be modest, but accuracy and case selection probably mature with experience. And even after accounting for case number, procedure time varied substantially between operators, which tells us that lesion complexity, staging, adjunct procedures, and workflow probably matter as much as raw experience.
Narrator: Let’s hit the methodology critique. What are the biggest limitations?
Guest: First, this was not randomized and did not have a direct comparator arm. We cannot say robotic bronchoscopy is superior to CT-guided biopsy, electromagnetic navigation, thin bronchoscopy, or any other diagnostic strategy from this study alone. Second, there is selection bias. The decision to use robotic bronchoscopy was made by the treating physician, so lesions chosen for robotic biopsy may have been lesions operators already thought were bronchoscopically approachable.
Narrator: Third, verification bias.
Guest: Yes. Not every benign result had surgical pathology. Many benign classifications depended on imaging stability or resolution. That is clinically reasonable, but it is not the same as universal tissue confirmation. There was also loss to follow-up. The CONSORT-style figure shows 151 lesions lost to follow-up, with additional exclusions for insufficient radiology follow-up, missing final histology, or incomplete procedures. Those exclusions can affect the final accuracy estimate.
Narrator: And generalizability?
Guest: This was a multi-hospital study, which helps, but it was still a single academic health system using one robotic platform. The cohort was predominantly White, and the procedures occurred in a system with thoracic surgery and pulmonary procedural expertise. Community programs, lower-volume centers, or centers without routine radial EBUS or integrated nodule conference workflows may see different outcomes.
Narrator: So how should an IP audience apply this?
Guest: The practical takeaway is not “robotic bronchoscopy for everything.” It is more selective. Robotic bronchoscopy appears to perform well for suspicious pulmonary lesions when there is favorable anatomy: larger size, solid morphology, bronchus sign, and radial EBUS localization. The low chest tube rate is a major advantage, especially in patients at higher risk from transthoracic biopsy.
Narrator: But for small, subsolid, bronchus-sign-negative, or anatomically difficult lesions, especially right upper lobe apical lesions, the procedure plan may need escalation.
Guest: Right. That could mean cone-beam CT, tomosynthesis-based confirmation, different sampling tools, alternative biopsy route, preoperative localization, or surgical diagnosis. And if the result is atypical or benign but discordant with clinical and radiographic suspicion, the algorithm should not stop.
Narrator: This paper also supports a multidisciplinary approach. More than half of cases included traditional EBUS, suggesting many patients underwent diagnosis and nodal staging in a single anesthetic event.
Guest: That is a real workflow advantage. For suspected lung cancer, the value of bronchoscopy is not only sampling the nodule. It is the ability to combine peripheral diagnosis, mediastinal staging, fiducial placement when needed, and sometimes other coordinated procedures. That systems-level benefit is hard to capture in a simple diagnostic accuracy number.
Narrator: Final synthesis: Deitz and colleagues provide a large, pragmatic, multi-operator look at Monarch robotic bronchoscopy in a lung nodule program. Diagnostic accuracy was 82.7 percent, chest tube rate was 3 percent, and performance was best in larger solid lesions with favorable imaging features and radial EBUS localization.
Guest: And the caveats are just as important: sensitivity for malignancy was lower than the overall accuracy figure, indeterminate results carried substantial malignant risk, and the study is observational with selection and verification bias. The paper supports robotic bronchoscopy as an important preferred bronchoscopic strategy for many suspicious pulmonary lesions, but it also argues for disciplined patient selection, imaging confirmation, and rigorous follow-up of nondiagnostic or atypical results.
