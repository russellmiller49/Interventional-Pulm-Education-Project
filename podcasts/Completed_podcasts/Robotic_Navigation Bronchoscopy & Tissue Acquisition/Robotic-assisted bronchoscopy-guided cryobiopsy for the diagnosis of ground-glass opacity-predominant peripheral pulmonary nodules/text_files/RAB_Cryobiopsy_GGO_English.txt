Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we’re discussing Zhang and colleagues, published in Respiratory Research in 2026, looking at shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy integrated with mobile cone-beam C T-guided cryobiopsy for ground-glass opacity-predominant peripheral pulmonary nodules.

Guest: And this is a very interventional pulmonology kind of question. G G O-predominant nodules are increasingly common, but they remain procedurally annoying. They’re often small, subtle on fluoroscopy, frequently lack a bronchus sign, and may represent anything from inflammation or focal fibrosis to atypical adenomatous hyperplasia or early adenocarcinoma.

Narrator: Right. And the article is asking whether combining several high-end tools — shape-sensing robotic bronchoscopy, mobile C B C T target confirmation, tunnel creation when needed, and a 1.1-millimeter cryoprobe — can produce a high diagnostic yield with acceptable safety in this difficult lesion category.

Guest: The key phrase there is “combined.” This is not simply robotic bronchoscopy. It is robotic bronchoscopy plus three-dimensional imaging, plus target update, plus tool-in-lesion confirmation, plus cryobiopsy as the primary sampling method. So the paper is really evaluating an advanced procedural workflow, not just a single device.

Narrator: The study design was retrospective, single center, and included consecutive patients who underwent this approach between November 2024 and June 2025. The population was selected for suspected malignant G G O-predominant peripheral pulmonary nodules, with consolidation-to-tumor ratio less than or equal to 0.5, and nodule diameter between 8 and 30 millimeters.

Guest: And the cohort was small, but clinically interesting: 40 patients with 55 nodules. Median nodule size in the largest dimension was 12 millimeters. About two-thirds were pure G G Os, about one-third were mixed G G Os, and most strikingly, 94.5 percent lacked a bronchus sign.

Narrator: That’s the part that gets the attention. For IP clinicians, lack of a bronchus sign is often where enthusiasm for bronchoscopy starts to fade. Here, the operators created a tunnel to the target lesion in 74.5 percent of nodules.

Guest: Exactly. Their Figure 1 is useful because it walks through the actual procedural logic. The robotic catheter is navigated toward the target bronchus, then mobile C B C T is used for target update. If the airway path is not ideal, a 21-gauge needle is used to puncture toward the lesion, C B C T confirms needle-in-lesion, the tract is dilated with the needle sheath, and then the cryoprobe is advanced through that tunnel. A second tool-in-lesion confirmation is done before freezing.

Narrator: And the sampling protocol was practical. Typically, 2 to 3 cryobiopsy samples were obtained, with a freeze duration of 3 to 4 seconds. The catheter was left in place while the cryoprobe and specimen were removed through the working channel, which the authors describe as helping with tamponade and repeat sampling without renavigation.

Guest: No forceps. No brush. No ROSE. That’s important. The diagnostic performance is really the performance of a cryobiopsy-centered workflow.

Narrator: So, what did they find? The per-nodule diagnostic yield was 87.3 percent, or 48 out of 55 nodules. Per-patient diagnostic yield was 87.5 percent. Diagnostic accuracy was also reported as 87.3 percent, and the false negative rate was 12.7 percent.

Guest: Diagnoses included 41 pulmonary adenocarcinomas, 5 atypical adenomatous hyperplasia lesions, and 2 lymphomas. Seven nodules were nondiagnostic, either nonspecific inflammation or normal pulmonary elements. And several of those nondiagnostic cases were later shown or presumed to be malignant by surgery, multidisciplinary discussion, ablation, or repeat robotic cryobiopsy.

Narrator: The subgroup results are also worth mentioning. Yield was 80 percent for nodules 10 millimeters or smaller, 90 percent for nodules larger than 10 millimeters, 83.3 percent for pure G G Os, 94.7 percent for mixed G G Os, and 86.5 percent for nodules without a bronchus sign. None of those subgroup comparisons were statistically significant.

Guest: Which is not surprising with this sample size. The study was not powered to detect meaningful subgroup differences. But as a descriptive signal, 86.5 percent yield in nodules without a bronchus sign is notable.

Narrator: Safety was favorable in this cohort. There were no major bleeding episodes, no pneumothorax, and no other reported procedure-related or device-related complications.

Guest: That’s reassuring, but we should be careful. Zero events in 40 patients and 55 nodules does not mean zero risk. It means this was feasible and apparently safe in a small cohort, performed by two experienced robotic bronchoscopists in a high-resource environment.

Narrator: Tissue adequacy is another important outcome. P D L one expression testing was attempted in 13 patients and was evaluable in 11, or 84.6 percent. Genetic testing was attempted in 16 cases, and all 16 had sufficient cryobiopsy specimens.

Guest: That may be one of the more clinically relevant points. For many G G O-predominant lesions, especially presumed early adenocarcinoma, the immediate question may be diagnosis before ablation, radiation, targeted therapy, or surgical decision-making. If the specimen supports immunohistochemistry and molecular analysis, that expands the clinical utility beyond “malignant versus benign.”

Narrator: Now let’s put the brakes on a little. The headline yield is impressive, but there are several methodological issues.

Guest: The first is the definition of diagnostic yield. The authors used the ATS and ACCP strict diagnostic yield framework, but with one modification: they counted atypical adenomatous hyperplasia as diagnostic. They justify that because A A H is premalignant and influenced clinical management in their cohort. That’s reasonable from a management standpoint, but it does inflate the diagnostic numerator compared with a stricter interpretation.

Narrator: If those 5 A A H cases were not counted as diagnostic, the numerator would drop from 48 to 43 out of 55. So listeners should mentally separate “diagnostic enough to guide management” from “strict malignant or specific benign diagnosis.”

Guest: That’s exactly the nuance. In G G O biology, A A H, adenocarcinoma in situ, minimally invasive adenocarcinoma, and lepidic-predominant invasive adenocarcinoma live on a spectrum. But for diagnostic yield studies, classification rules matter a lot.

Narrator: Second, this was not a comparative study. There was no conventional robotic biopsy arm, no forceps-first arm, no needle-only arm, no transthoracic biopsy arm, and no surgery-first strategy.

Guest: So we cannot say the 87 percent yield was caused by cryobiopsy, or by robotic bronchoscopy, or by C B C T, or by tunneling. The result is the output of the entire bundled workflow. That is clinically useful, but it limits causal interpretation.

Narrator: Third, there is verification bias. Surgical confirmation was limited. Many patients proceeded to ablation or other management pathways, and follow-up was at least 6 months. The authors tried to be conservative by classifying all negative diagnoses as false negatives, which helps avoid underestimating false negatives, but this still is not the same as universal surgical pathology.

Guest: And that matters especially for G G Os, where growth kinetics can be slow. Six months is helpful, but it is not long-term biological confirmation for every lesion.

Narrator: Fourth, generalizability is a major question. This was single center, performed by two bronchoscopists with substantial robotic bronchoscopy experience. The procedure also required mobile C B C T integration, multiple scans, robotic planning, cryobiopsy equipment, and team familiarity with tunnel creation.

Guest: Right. This is not just adding a cryoprobe to a routine bronchoscopy cart. The hidden workflow includes anesthesia, radiation management, room logistics, C B C T acquisition and reconstruction, target update, tool confirmation, and the judgment to create a parenchymal tunnel without causing bleeding that compromises subsequent sampling.

Narrator: Procedure time was also not trivial. Median procedure time was 64.5 minutes, with an interquartile range from 45.0 to 85.8 minutes.

Guest: And radiation deserves discussion. The authors note that at least 3 to 4 mobile C B C T scans were performed per patient: after navigation for target update, after needle puncture when tunneling was needed, after cryoprobe placement to confirm tool-in-lesion, and at the end to assess bleeding or pneumothorax. That is defensible in a feasibility study, but it is a real implementation issue.

Narrator: So how should an IP audience translate this?

Guest: This paper supports the idea that for selected G G O-predominant nodules, especially lesions without a bronchus sign, a C B C T-confirmed robotic cryobiopsy workflow can achieve high diagnostic yield and may provide tissue adequate for downstream testing. That is relevant for patients who are not surgical candidates, patients being considered for ablation or radiation, and patients where tissue confirmation is needed before committing to a definitive local therapy.

Narrator: But it should not be read as evidence that every persistent G G O requires biopsy, or that this approach should replace surgery in operable patients, or that every center with a robot can reproduce the same yield.

Guest: Exactly. The bigger clinical question is not only “can we biopsy these lesions?” Increasingly, the question is “should we biopsy this particular lesion, in this particular patient, at this point in the natural history?” For indolent pure G G Os, procedural success can still lead to overdiagnosis, overtreatment, or anxiety-driven escalation.

Narrator: That comes through in the A A H cases, where surveillance was initially recommended, but patients ultimately underwent ablation after multidisciplinary discussion because of anxiety about progression.

Guest: That’s a very real scenario. It also highlights why multidisciplinary framing matters. A highly capable biopsy platform does not remove the need for careful nodule board judgment.

Narrator: The study also fits into the broader trend in advanced bronchoscopy: diagnostic yield is increasingly dependent on confirmation and correction. Robotic stability helps, but C T-to-body divergence still has to be solved. C B C T helps solve that. Cryobiopsy may improve sample quality. Tunneling may help overcome the absent bronchus sign.

Guest: And the devil’s advocate response is: when all of those things are combined, which part is essential? Could a similar yield be achieved with robotic needle biopsy plus C B C T? Is cryobiopsy adding incremental value, or mostly tissue volume? Is the tunnel technique the major differentiator? Those are the next studies.

Narrator: Ideally, future work would be multicenter, prospective, and comparative. It would include standardized definitions of diagnostic yield, longer follow-up for negative and premalignant findings, confidence intervals around yield and complication rates, radiation dose reporting, and perhaps comparisons between needle, forceps, and cryobiopsy strategies under C B C T confirmation.

Guest: And implementation outcomes too: procedure time, room turnover, anesthesia time, costs, learning curve, number of C B C T spins, and failure modes. Those matter if this is going to move from expert-center innovation to broader practice.

Narrator: So, final synthesis. Zhang and colleagues provide a compelling single-center signal that shape-sensing robotic bronchoscopy integrated with mobile C B C T-guided cryobiopsy can achieve high diagnostic yield for small G G O-predominant peripheral pulmonary nodules, many of which lacked a bronchus sign, with no major complications observed in this cohort.

Guest: The article is encouraging, technically sophisticated, and highly relevant to advanced IP programs. But it remains retrospective, highly selected, noncomparative, and operator-dependent. It should generate interest and further study, not unqualified practice change.

Narrator: For the practicing interventional pulmonologist, the take-home is this: G G O-predominant nodules may be increasingly bronchoscopically accessible when robotic navigation, C B C T confirmation, tunneling techniques, and cryobiopsy are combined. But patient selection, multidisciplinary decision-making, and honest accounting of workflow and generalizability remain central.

Guest: Well said. The technology may help us reach the lesion. The clinical judgment is still deciding whether reaching it helps the patient.
