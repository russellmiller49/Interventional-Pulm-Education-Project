Narrator: Welcome to Interventional Pulm dot com I P Journal Review. Today we are discussing a paper by Chen-Yost and colleagues, published in the Journal of Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology in twenty twenty-six, titled “Accuracy of Rapid On-Site Evaluation in Robotic-Assisted Bronchoscopy Fine Needle Aspirations of Lung Nodules.”
Guest:  And this is a very practical paper. Rose during robotic bronchoscopy sits right at the intersection of diagnostic confidence, procedural workflow, cytopathology staffing, radiation exposure, and tissue acquisition strategy. The question is not just, “Can Rose tell us we are in the lesion?” It is also, “What should we do with that information once we have it?”
Narrator: Exactly. The authors are looking at shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy, or S S R A B, with fine needle aspiration of lung nodules. The specific aim was to evaluate concordance between preliminary Rose cytology, final cytology, and histopathologic biopsy. They also looked at number of passes, lesion characteristics, and biopsy tool type—especially forceps versus cryoprobe.
Guest:  The framing matters because a lot of prior robotic bronchoscopy literature has focused on diagnostic yield. That is useful, but it can flatten the clinical nuance. A positive malignant Rose result, a negative Rose result, and an atypical or non-diagnostic Rose result are not equivalent signals. This paper tries to separate those categories.
Narrator: Study design first. This was a single-institution retrospective study of lung nodules sampled by S S R A B, F N A, with Rose. Cases were collected from November twenty twenty-two through March twenty twenty-four. All procedures used the ION platform, with C T-based planning, radial E-bus confirmation after navigation, F N A, and then additional biopsies at operator discretion using either forceps, a one point one millimeter cryoprobe, or both. Importantly, sampling was guided by two-D fluoroscopy, without C B C T or digital tomosynthesis.
Guest:  That last point is key for generalizability. Many I P programs now pair robotic bronchoscopy with C B C T or tomosynthesis, and the role of Rose may shift when you have more robust tool-in-lesion confirmation. In this study, Rose may have been carrying more workflow value because they were not using advanced intra-procedural imaging.
Narrator: The cohort included two hundred forty-nine F N A cytology specimens from two hundred thirty-four patients, with two hundred two concurrent histopathologic biopsies. The average lesion size was one point eight centimeters. About half the lesions were peripheral. Radial E-bus visualization was achieved in roughly ninety-one to ninety-two percent of cases. Biopsy tools were fairly balanced: ninety-four forceps biopsies, ninety-eight cryobiopsies, and ten cases with both.
Guest:  And the headline results are clinically memorable. Overall diagnostic yield was sixty-one percent for cytology and eighty-one percent for histopathologic biopsy. For peripheral lesions, final cytology yield was fifty-eight percent, while histopathologic biopsy yield was eighty-four percent. That gap alone reinforces something most proceduralists already suspect: F N A cytology and histologic tissue are doing different jobs.
Narrator: Right. F N A may be fast and useful for Rose, but histologic tissue remains central for diagnosis, architecture, ancillary testing, and increasingly for molecular adequacy. The authors found that diagnostic yield improved with larger lesion size, although that trend was not statistically significant. Lesion size, lobe, segment, peripheral versus central location, radial E-bus visualization, and proceduralist did not significantly affect cytology diagnosis.
Guest:  The number of passes did matter. Diagnostic yield plateaued after three F N A passes for Rose, and Figure one makes this visually intuitive: suspicious or positive cases cluster in the first few passes, while atypical or non-diagnostic cases become more prominent as the number of passes increases. In other words, if the lesion is going to declare itself cytologically as malignant, it often does so early.
Narrator: That is probably one of the most actionable workflow points in the paper. Three passes may be a reasonable ceiling for Rose-directed adequacy assessment. But the nuance is that three passes for Rose is not the same as three total passes for the case. If additional material is needed for cell block, molecular testing, P D L 1, flow cytometry, microbiology, or other ancillary studies, those are separate tissue acquisition goals.
Guest:  Agreed. A bad interpretation would be: “Only do three passes total.” A better interpretation is: “After three Rose passes, continuing to make more smears may have diminishing returns; consider redirecting sampling toward cell block or histologic tissue.”
Narrator: The concordance analysis is where the paper is most interesting. Positive Rose results were highly reliable. The authors report that a positive preliminary Rose diagnosis for malignancy correlated strongly with positive histopathology—the abstract reports eighty-six percent correlation—and the category-level breakdown shows very high concordance between suspicious or positive Rose and final cytology, essentially ninety-nine percent.
Guest:  That is the comforting part. A positive malignant Rose during S S R A B seems to be a strong signal. But the study also shows that negative and non-diagnostic Rose are much less reassuring. Negative final cytology correlated with negative histopathology only sixty-one percent of the time, and fourteen percent of those negative final cytology cases were suspicious or positive on histopathology. For atypical or non-diagnostic cytology, the final histology distribution was almost evenly split among atypical or non-diagnostic, negative, and suspicious or positive categories.
Narrator: That is the cautionary message. A positive Rose can help confirm that the procedure is on the right track. But a negative or non-diagnostic Rose should not be used as a procedural stop sign in a high-risk lesion. It should probably trigger more thoughtful sampling: reassess imaging, reconfirm radial E-bus, consider tool-in-lesion limitations, and obtain histologic tissue if it is safe.
Guest:  And that brings us to forceps versus cryobiopsy. Cryobiopsies had an eighty-seven percent diagnostic tissue yield compared with seventy-four percent for forceps biopsies, and forceps were more likely to produce atypical or non-diagnostic histology, with a P value of zero point zero four.
Narrator: Mechanistically, that makes sense. Forceps often need an en face approach and can cause crush artifact. Cryoprobes may sample tangentially or circumferentially, obtain larger tissue volume, and retrieve fibrotic or cohesive tissue better than forceps. But this was not randomized. Tool choice was at operator discretion, so selection bias is a real issue.
Guest:  Exactly. Were cryoprobes used later in the study period, after more team experience? Were they selected for certain lesion morphologies? Were forceps used in cases where the airway orientation was less favorable? The paper does not fully resolve that. The finding is clinically plausible, but it is not definitive comparative effectiveness evidence.
Narrator: Safety is worth mentioning. Nineteen patients had complications, about eight percent. Pneumothorax occurred in nine cases, and non-massive bleeding in eight. There was no mortality during the study period. But the study was not designed to compare safety by biopsy tool in a randomized way, so we should be careful not to conclude too much about cryobiopsy safety relative to forceps from these data alone.
Guest:  Methodologically, the biggest limitation is that the study evaluates concordance, not the clinical impact of Rose versus no Rose. Every case had Rose available. So this paper cannot tell us whether Rose improves diagnostic yield, reduces repeat procedures, shortens cases, or improves molecular adequacy. It tells us how Rose results behave when compared with final cytology and histology.
Narrator: Another limitation is verification bias. Not every nodule had concurrent histopathologic biopsy or surgical confirmation. Some had clinical follow-up, and some had limited follow-up. In lung nodule diagnostic studies, that matters because the reference standard can vary depending on whether the result was positive, negative, or indeterminate.
Guest:  And Rose itself has inherent limitations. It relies on rapid preparation, often air-dried slides, with less material than final cytology or histology. The authors mention artifacts such as enlarged nuclei, and they did not capture lesion heterogeneity, vascularity, or needle size. Those variables could easily influence whether F N A cytology performs well.
Narrator: So how should an I P audience translate this?
•	First, a positive Rose during S S R A B is meaningful. It should increase confidence that the lesion has been sampled, but it should not distract from collecting enough tissue for histology and ancillary testing.
•	Second, negative or non-diagnostic Rose should not reassure the team too much. In a suspicious lesion, that result should push the operator toward additional sampling strategy rather than premature closure. That may mean cryobiopsy, repeat radial E-bus assessment, additional fluoroscopic views, or advanced imaging where available.
•	Third, three Rose-directed F N A passes may be a practical stopping point for smear adequacy. After that, if the answer is still atypical or non-diagnostic, more smears may mostly add time, blood, radiation exposure, and interpretive difficulty. The next move may be a different specimen type, not just more of the same.
•	And fourth, the cryobiopsy signal is important but not final. This study supports the growing impression that peripheral cryobiopsy can improve histologic yield in robotic bronchoscopy workflows, but prospective data are still needed to sort out lesion selection, bleeding risk, tool sequencing, molecular adequacy, and cost.
Guest:  The broader workflow question remains unresolved: which cases truly need Rose? If a center has C B C T confirmation, experienced operators, reliable cryobiopsy, and strong cell block processing, Rose may have less marginal value. But for heterogeneous lesions, cavitary wall targets, suspected malignancy where immediate adequacy changes sampling behavior, or cases where navigation confidence is uncertain, Rose may still be useful.
Narrator: That’s where this paper is helpful. It does not say Rose is universally necessary or unnecessary. It says the meaning of Rose depends heavily on the diagnostic category. Positive is strong. Negative is weaker. Non-diagnostic is not neutral; it is a warning that the sampling strategy may need adjustment.
Guest:  Final synthesis: Chen-Yost and colleagues provide a granular look at Rose concordance in S S R A B lung nodule sampling. The most useful takeaways are that positive malignant Rose is highly concordant with final diagnosis, three Rose passes appear sufficient for cytologic adequacy in many malignant cases, and histologic cryobiopsy outperformed forceps biopsy in diagnostic tissue yield in this retrospective cohort.
Narrator: But the study should not be overread. It is single-center, retrospective, without a no-Rose comparator, without C B C T, and with nonrandomized biopsy tool selection. The right conclusion is not “Rose changes practice,” but rather “Rose results should be interpreted differently by category, and procedural workflow should adapt accordingly.”
Guest:  That wraps up this episode of Interventional Pulm dot com I P Journal Review. The practical question for the procedure suite is not simply whether Rose is present. It is whether the team knows when Rose should change the next move—and when it should not.
