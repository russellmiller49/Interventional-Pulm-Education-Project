Narrator: Welcome to Interventional Pulm dot com I.P. Journal Review, where we take a focused look at clinically relevant papers for interventional pulmonology. Today we’re discussing Thiboutot and colleagues, published in J.A.M.A. in twenty twenty-six, looking at one point one-millimeter cryoprobe transbronchial biopsy versus conventional forceps biopsy: the Frostbite-two randomized clinical trial.
Guest: This is a very practical question. Bronchoscopists have spent a lot of time improving navigation—robotic platforms, cone-beam C.T., augmented fluoroscopy, radial E-bus—but once the tool is actually at the target, tissue acquisition is still the bottleneck. Forceps are familiar, cheap, and easy to use, but small specimens and crush artifact are persistent problems.
Narrator: Exactly. And this paper asks whether the smaller one point one-millimeter cryoprobe improves diagnostic yield compared with two point zero-millimeter forceps, without recreating the safety concerns associated with older, larger transbronchial cryobiopsy platforms.
Guest: The distinction matters. This is not the classic I.L.D. cryobiopsy paradigm with a larger cryoprobe, en bloc bronchoscope removal, prophylactic blockers, and a very different bleeding-management workflow. The one point one-millimeter cryoprobe is passed through the working channel, and the bronchoscope remains in place. That changes the procedural risk profile and makes the comparison to forceps much more relevant for routine bronchoscopic biopsy.
Narrator: So let’s walk through the design. Frostbite-two was an open-label, outcome assessor–masked, multicenter randomized clinical trial conducted at nine U.S. medical centers. They randomized five hundred adults undergoing clinically indicated transbronchial biopsy for one of three indications: lung nodules or masses, lung transplant evaluation, or diffuse parenchymal lung disease.
Guest: Randomization was one to one to either the one point one-millimeter cryoprobe or standard two point zero-millimeter forceps, stratified by indication. Importantly, for pulmonary nodules and masses, patients were randomized intraprocedurally once the decision had been made to perform transbronchial biopsy. Patients who already got a diagnosis by another modality during bronchoscopy, such as T.B.N.A., were not randomized.
Narrator: That’s an important nuance. The study is not asking, “What is the total diagnostic yield of bronchoscopy?” It is asking, “When transbronchial biopsy is being performed, does the biopsy tool itself affect histologic diagnostic yield?”
Guest: Right. And the primary endpoint was diagnostic yield based on the transbronchial biopsy sample alone, assessed by a central pathology core that was masked to the biopsy method. That is a real strength. It reduces the noise of local pathology interpretation and keeps the endpoint tied to the actual randomized tissue-acquisition method.
Narrator: They also standardized biopsy number. Lung transplant patients had ten transbronchial biopsy specimens collected, consistent with I.S.H.L.T.-style sampling expectations, while lung nodule or mass and diffuse parenchymal lung disease patients had five specimens obtained.
Guest: Procedurally, this was pragmatic. Bronchoscopists could use fluoroscopy, radial E-bus, electromagnetic navigation, robotic bronchoscopy, cone-beam C.T., and other localization methods according to local practice. So the trial did not isolate the biopsy tool in an artificial vacuum. It asked how the tool performs inside contemporary bronchoscopy workflows.
Narrator: Now the headline result: among the four hundred ninety patients included in the primary analysis, diagnostic yield was eighty-eight point six percent with cryobiopsy versus seventy-eight point eight percent with forceps. That’s an absolute difference of nine point eight percentage points, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval from three point three to sixteen point three, and a P-value of point zero zero three.
Guest: Clinically, a roughly ten-point absolute increase is not trivial. If that holds up across real-world practice, that could mean fewer nondiagnostic bronchoscopies, fewer repeat procedures, faster oncologic decision-making, and potentially less downstream C.T.-guided biopsy or surgical biopsy.
Narrator: But the subgroup results are where the discussion gets more interesting. For lung nodules or masses, yield was eighty-three point two percent with cryoprobe versus seventy point one percent with forceps—absolute difference thirteen point one percentage points, P equals point zero four. For lung transplant, yield was ninety-six point zero percent versus eighty-eight point seven percent, absolute difference seven point three points, P equals point zero three. For diffuse parenchymal lung disease, yield was seventy-two point zero percent versus sixty-two point five percent, but the confidence interval was wide, from negative sixteen point zero to positive thirty-three point six, and the P-value was point five five.
Guest: That diffuse parenchymal lung disease subgroup was small—twenty-five assigned to cryoprobe and twenty-five to forceps, with forty-nine included in that subgroup analysis. So the negative result there should not be read as proof of no benefit. It is more that this trial does not resolve the I.L.D. question, especially given that the one point one-millimeter probe is a different tool from the larger cryoprobes used in much of the I.L.D. literature.
Narrator: And for transplant, “diagnostic yield” has to be interpreted carefully. In the allograft group, A zero through A four rejection grading counted as diagnostic, while A X or inadequate tissue counted as nondiagnostic. So this is largely about adequacy for rejection assessment, not necessarily identifying more rejection events.
Guest: That’s a key point. A higher yield in transplant could still matter operationally, because inadequate samples create ambiguity and sometimes repeat procedures. But it is not the same endpoint as cancer diagnosis or molecular adequacy.
Narrator: Speaking of tissue adequacy, the histologic secondary outcomes support the biological plausibility. Cryobiopsy produced larger samples, more alveolated area, less crush artifact, and higher histologic accessibility scores. Median total specimen area was sixteen point seven square millimeters with cryoprobe versus nine point six with forceps. Median total alveolated area was thirteen point one versus six point four square millimeters. Median crush artifact was zero percent versus five percent.
Guest: That’s probably the most intuitive explanation for the diagnostic yield difference. The cryoprobe is not magic; it is getting more intact tissue. For peripheral nodules, that could be the difference between atypia, benign parenchyma, or crush artifact on one hand, and a clear malignant diagnosis on the other.
Narrator: Now, safety. This is where many people’s ears perk up whenever cryobiopsy is mentioned. The prespecified composite safety endpoint—pneumothorax requiring drainage, grade three or higher bleeding, respiratory failure, or death—occurred in zero patients in the cryoprobe group versus four patients, or one point six percent, in the forceps group.
Guest: And all six pneumothoraces in the study occurred in the forceps group, with four requiring chest tube placement. There were no grade three or four bleeding events, no respiratory failure events, and no deaths in either group. Lower-grade bleeding was not absent—grade one bleeding occurred in twenty-five point six percent with cryoprobe and twenty-one point six percent with forceps, and grade two bleeding in six point eight percent versus seven point six percent—but clinically significant bleeding did not appear increased with the one point one-millimeter cryoprobe.
Narrator: The temptation is to say the cryoprobe is safer, but that would be too strong. The trial was powered for diagnostic yield, not rare safety events. With two hundred fifty patients per arm, zero major bleeding events is reassuring, but it does not exclude uncommon complications, especially in less controlled settings.
Guest: Agreed. Also, these were large academic centers, with bronchoscopists who had at least some cryoprobe training and procedural volume. The paper notes participating centers performed at least one hundred transbronchial biopsies annually and had affiliated lung cancer, transplant, and I.L.D. programs. That improves trial execution, but it may limit generalizability.
Narrator: Another practical detail: most cases were done through an L.M.A. or endotracheal tube. Rigid bronchoscopy was used in only point six percent, and prophylactic bronchial blockers in only point four percent. That supports the idea that one point one-millimeter cryobiopsy can be incorporated into standard flexible bronchoscopy workflows rather than requiring an I.L.D.-style cryobiopsy setup.
Guest: But implementation still has barriers. The disposable cryobiopsy equipment costs more than forceps, and this study did not evaluate cost-effectiveness. So the relevant question for a bronchoscopy unit is not just, “Does it increase yield?” It is, “Does the increased yield offset device cost, procedure time, training, and workflow changes by reducing repeat procedures or accelerating care?”
Narrator: And for lung nodules, the answer may depend heavily on the existing platform. A center using robotic bronchoscopy plus cone-beam C.T. and already achieving high diagnostic yield may see a smaller marginal benefit than a center struggling with tissue adequacy. Conversely, if navigation is excellent but histology is frequently limited by small samples or crush artifact, this tool may be very attractive.
Guest: There is also the molecular testing question. The study focused on histologic diagnosis. It did not directly answer whether cryobiopsy improves adequacy for N.G.S., P.D. L. one, or broader biomarker testing. Given the larger, less crushed samples, it is reasonable to hypothesize benefit, but that was not the endpoint.
Narrator: That matters because in thoracic oncology, “diagnostic” is no longer enough. A sample that says adenocarcinoma but cannot support molecular profiling may still delay treatment. Future studies should probably look at full diagnostic sufficiency: histology, immunohistochemistry, molecular testing, and maybe time to treatment.
Guest: Another limitation is missing data. Ten patients were excluded from the primary analysis because definitive histology material was unavailable for central review. Overall sensitivity analyses did not change the primary result, but in the lung nodule or mass subgroup, if all missing data in the cryoprobe group were imputed as nondiagnostic, that subgroup lost statistical significance.
Narrator: So the overall result is robust, but the nodule subgroup should be interpreted with some caution. The direction of effect is clinically compelling, but the P-value is close to the threshold and the confidence interval starts at one point zero percentage point.
Guest: Also, because the trial was open-label, bronchoscopists knew which tool they were using. The central pathologists were masked, which protects the primary endpoint, but operator behavior could still differ. For example, subtle differences in positioning, confidence, or persistence might influence sampling.
Narrator: On the other hand, the number of biopsies was standardized, and the pragmatic nature of the trial is a strength. This is not a hyper-controlled bench study. It reflects how these procedures are actually performed: different localization tools, different airway choices, local procedural preferences, but a randomized tissue-acquisition device.
Guest: The industry funding also deserves mention. The study was funded by Erbe Elektromedizin, and multiple authors reported disclosures. The manuscript states the funder had no role in design, conduct, analysis, or publication decisions. That does not invalidate the findings, but it is part of critical appraisal, especially for device trials.
Narrator: So how should an interventional pulmonologist apply this? For pulmonary nodules and masses, this trial supports considering the one point one-millimeter cryoprobe as a tissue acquisition tool when transbronchial biopsy is being performed, particularly when sample size and crush artifact are recurring limitations.
Guest: For lung transplant bronchoscopy, the data suggest improved adequacy for histologic grading, but programs would need to decide whether that yield gain justifies cost and training, especially if their current A X rates are already low.
Narrator: For diffuse parenchymal lung disease, this paper should not be used as a broad endorsement of replacing established I.L.D. cryobiopsy strategies or surgical lung biopsy decision-making. The subgroup was small, and the one point one-millimeter probe may not provide the same architectural sample as larger cryoprobes. It is hypothesis-generating in that space.
Guest: And across all indications, patient selection still matters. The trial excluded patients with standard contraindications to transbronchial biopsy: significant bleeding disorder, platelets below fifty thousand, inability to hold relevant anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy aside from aspirin monotherapy, and D.N.R. or D.N.I. status. So safety should not be extrapolated to higher-risk populations without caution.
Narrator: The take-home is balanced but meaningful. Frostbite-two provides randomized evidence that one point one-millimeter cryoprobe transbronchial biopsy improves histologic diagnostic yield compared with forceps, with better tissue quality and no observed increase in major complications in this trial.
Guest: The remaining questions are cost-effectiveness, molecular adequacy, performance in lower-volume centers, durability of safety data across broader practice, and exactly which patient groups benefit most.
Narrator: Well said. For I.P. clinicians, this is less about replacing every forceps biopsy tomorrow and more about recognizing that the tissue acquisition tool may be as important as the navigation platform. Getting to the lesion is only half the procedure. Getting usable tissue is the other half.
Guest: And this trial gives the field a much stronger evidence base for that second half.
