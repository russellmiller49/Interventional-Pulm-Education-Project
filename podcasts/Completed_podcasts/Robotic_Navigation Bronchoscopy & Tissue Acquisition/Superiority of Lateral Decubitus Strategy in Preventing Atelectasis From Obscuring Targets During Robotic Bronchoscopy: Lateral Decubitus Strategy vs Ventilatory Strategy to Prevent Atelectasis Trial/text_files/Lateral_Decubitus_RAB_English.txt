Narrator: Welcome to Interventional Pulm dot com, I P Journal Review. Today we’re discussing Boster and colleagues, published in CHEST in 2026, looking at a very practical procedural question: for peripheral nodules in dependent lung zones, is lateral decubitus positioning better than a ventilatory strategy for preventing atelectasis from obscuring the target during robotic bronchoscopy?
Guest: And this is one of those papers where the question feels immediately recognizable to anyone doing advanced bronchoscopy with C B C T. The robot may navigate beautifully, the virtual pathway may look reasonable, and then the intraoperative spin shows the problem: dependent atelectasis has swallowed the lesion, or at least distorted the anatomy enough that “tool-in-lesion” becomes hard to interpret.
Narrator: Right. The paper frames atelectasis not just as an imaging nuisance, but as a cause of C T-to-body divergence, false-positive radial E B U S, and sometimes complete target obscuration. The comparator here is important. They’re not comparing lateral positioning with sloppy ventilation. They’re comparing LADS ... lateral decubitus strategy ... against VESPA, a structured ventilatory strategy using recruitment, titrated F I O 2, PEEP, and tidal volumes based on ideal body weight.
Guest: Exactly. VESPA had already shown it could reduce atelectasis, but not eliminate it. The core hypothesis here is almost gravitational: if the lesion is in a dependent zone when the patient is supine, make the target lung nondependent. Turn the patient lateral decubitus with the lesion side up, and maybe you prevent the very atelectasis that would otherwise obscure the lesion.
Narrator: Let’s walk through the design. This was a prospective randomized controlled trial, one-to-one randomization, enrolling adult patients undergoing robotic bronchoscopy for peripheral nodules up to 3 centimeters, suspicious for malignancy, located in dependent lung zones. The definition of “dependent” was not casual. More than 50 percent of the nodule volume had to fall below a horizontal line drawn at the anterior edge of the vertebral body on axial C T ... what they describe as high-risk zones for atelectasis. The page 4 figure is useful here because it visually defines those dependent zones.
Guest: And the exclusions matter. They excluded pure ground-glass nodules, consolidation, pregnancy, pleural effusions, ascites, diaphragmatic paralysis, severe air-trapping with residual volume above 150 percent, and shoulder injury on the side that would be down during lateral decubitus. So this is not “all comers” robotic bronchoscopy. It’s a selected high-risk atelectasis population.
Narrator: The intervention was also very procedural. In VESPA, patients were intubated supine and ventilated with V T 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram I B W, F I O 2 less than 100 percent titrated to maintain saturation above 94 percent, PEEP 8 to 10, plus a recruitment maneuver: 10 breaths at plateau pressure 40 with PEEP 20.
Guest: In the LADS arm, they used V T 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram I B W, F I O 2 100 percent, and low PEEP ... 0 to 5. After intubation and airway inspection, patients were turned lateral decubitus with the target side up, secured with a bean bag and foam straps. Figure 3 on page 5 is helpful because it shows the practical room setup: patient lateral, robot at the head, C B C T C-arm positioned around that geometry. It’s a reminder that this is not just a ventilation paper; it’s a workflow paper.
Narrator: Primary endpoint was development of atelectasis obscuring the target on mobile C B C T. And they used a clinically relevant threshold: atelectasis obscuring more than 50 percent of the target volume, enough to preclude sampling. Secondary endpoints were tool-in-lesion, diagnostic yield on index biopsy using A T S / CHEST consensus-type diagnostic criteria, and safety.
Guest: The results are pretty striking. They analyzed 62 patients: 29 in LADS, 33 in VESPA. No patients in the LADS group developed atelectasis obscuring the target. In the VESPA group, 9 of 33 did ... 27.3 percent, with a 95 percent C I of 13.3 percent to 45.5 percent, P equals point zero zero two.
Narrator: That’s the headline. And then the procedural outcomes follow. Tool-in-lesion was achieved in 29 of 29 patients in LADS ... 100 percent ... compared with 24 of 33 in VESPA, or 72.7 percent, P equals point zero zero five. Diagnostic yield on index biopsy was 86.2 percent in LADS versus 57.6 percent in VESPA, P equals point zero two six.
Guest: The crossover data are also clinically interesting. All 9 patients in VESPA who developed target-obscuring atelectasis crossed over to lateral decubitus. Atelectasis was eliminated in all 9, tool-in-lesion was achieved in all 9, and diagnosis was obtained in 7 of 9.
Narrator: Figure 4 on page 5 is probably the most persuasive visual in the paper. It shows the preprocedural left lower lobe target, then C B C T with atelectasis obscuring more than half the target during VESPA, and then elimination of atelectasis with tool-in-lesion after crossover to LADS. That figure makes the mechanism very tangible.
Guest: Now, the devil’s advocate point: some of the secondary outcome difference is structurally linked to the primary endpoint. Per protocol, if the target was obscured, they considered the case no T I L and nondiagnostic in the original randomized arm. That is clinically reasonable ... you shouldn’t sample blindly when you can’t confirm the lesion ... but statistically it makes T I L and diagnostic yield downstream of the same phenomenon.
Narrator: Right. So the T I L and yield differences are important, but they are not independent signals. They’re the procedural consequences of target obscuration. That said, in real bronchoscopy, those consequences are exactly what matters. If the target disappears on C B C T, the case has changed.
Guest: Another thing I liked was the patient mix. These were not easy lesions. Median target size was 1.6 centimeters. Median distance to pleura was 0.6 centimeters. More than 87 percent were lower-lobe targets, and only 22.6 percent had a bronchus sign. So this is the population where atelectasis can make a technically demanding case become nondiagnostic.
Narrator: And higher B M I predicted atelectasis obscuring the target within the VESPA arm: O R 1.21 per 1-unit B M I increase, 95 percent C I 1.03 to 1.42. That fits prior physiologic intuition. For case selection, the patient with a posterior or dependent lower-lobe nodule and elevated B M I is exactly the patient where supine ventilation alone may not be enough.
Guest: The workflow tradeoff is real, though. Intubation-to-robot docking time was longer with LADS: median 18 minutes versus 10 minutes with VESPA, P less than point zero zero one. But docking-to-undocking time, total intubation-to-extubation time, fluoroscopy time, number of C B C T spins, and dose-area product were not significantly different.
Narrator: That’s a key operational point. Lateral positioning added setup complexity, especially isocentering and collision checks, but it did not appear to prolong the robotic portion or increase radiation in this study. Still, centers trying this need to think about room layout, C-arm geometry, anesthesia access, pressure-point protection, shoulder limitations, and team choreography.
Guest: The safety data were reassuring, at least in this sample. No deaths, no major complications, and no difference in minor complications. Two grade two bleeds occurred in the LADS group, one pneumothorax in each arm, and three patients in each arm required a single vasopressor dose.
Narrator: Methodologically, the strengths are clear: randomized design, clinically meaningful comparator, high-risk lesion selection, m C B C T confirmation, and a primary endpoint that matters procedurally. But there are limitations. It was a single institution, even though procedures were done by seven operators across three hospitals. The sample was small. Blinding was impossible for positioning, but the bigger issue is that atelectasis obscuring the target was judged by the proceduralist rather than independent blinded adjudicators.
Guest: And the authors acknowledge that post hoc adjudication wasn’t possible because some images had been erased from the server. That matters because the primary endpoint has a subjective component. Is it 40 percent obscured, 60 percent obscured, or functionally impossible to biopsy? In a live case, the operator has to decide. But for trial validity, independent image review would have strengthened the result.
Narrator: Generalizability is another issue. This trial was done with shape-sensing robotic bronchoscopy and mobile C B C T in a highly capable environment. It doesn’t necessarily tell us what happens with other robotic platforms, fixed C B C T rooms, tomosynthesis-only workflows, fluoroscopy-only workflows, or centers without routine intraoperative 3 D imaging.
Guest: And it does not say that every robotic bronchoscopy should be lateral. That would be overreach. The relevant population is dependent-zone nodules at meaningful risk of atelectasis, especially lower-lobe and posterior lesions, and perhaps higher B M I patients. For anterior, upper-lobe, nondependent targets, the benefit may be minimal while the workflow burden remains.
Narrator: The comparison with prone positioning is interesting too. The authors discuss prone bronchoscopy as another positional strategy, but note that prone positioning may be more cumbersome and potentially riskier in a bronchoscopy lab, especially if there is bleeding, pneumothorax, or airway instability. Lateral decubitus is familiar from pleuroscopy-style positioning and may be more manageable.
Guest: For implementation, the practical question is whether LADS should be planned upfront or used as a rescue. This study supports planned LADS for selected dependent lesions. But the crossover data also suggest a rescue pathway: if C B C T shows target-obscuring atelectasis in supine positioning despite optimized ventilation, turning lateral with target side up may salvage the case.
Narrator: That rescue pathway may be one of the most immediately actionable takeaways. It requires that the team be prepared before the case starts. Turning an anesthetized patient with a robot, C B C T, and sterile field constraints is not something to improvise casually.
Guest: Agreed. The anesthesia implications are also not trivial. LADS used lower PEEP, avoiding the hemodynamic burden of more aggressive recruitment strategies. But lateral positioning changes access, pressure points, tube security, and imaging constraints. The whole team has to buy into the workflow.
Narrator: So, what does this article add? It gives randomized evidence that for small peripheral nodules in dependent lung zones, lateral decubitus positioning with the target side up can prevent target-obscuring atelectasis better than a structured ventilatory strategy. It also shows associated improvements in tool-in-lesion and index diagnostic yield in that selected population.
Guest: What remains uncertain is reproducibility outside this environment, performance across platforms, whether independent blinded adjudication would produce the same endpoint classification, and how broadly to apply LADS beyond the specific high-risk lesion population studied.
Narrator: Bottom line for the I P audience: this is not a blanket mandate to turn every robotic bronchoscopy lateral. But for a dependent lower-lobe lesion, especially small, pleural-adjacent, bronchus-sign-negative, or in a higher B M I patient, this paper makes a strong argument that positioning deserves as much attention as ventilation, navigation software, and sampling tools.
Guest: And maybe the simplest framing is this: when the target is likely to drown in dependent atelectasis, don’t just recruit the lung ... consider moving the target out of dependency.
