InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review: Transbronchial Radiofrequency Ablation for Lung Tumors
Estimated duration: approximately 9 to 10 minutes
Article type: Original research - prospective, multicenter, open-label, single-arm device and procedural outcome study.

Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com I P Journal Review. In this episode, we are looking at Zhong and colleagues, published in Respirology in 2025, examining the safety and efficacy of a novel transbronchial radiofrequency ablation system for lung tumors, with one-year follow-up from the BRONC R F two clinical trial.

Guest: This is very much in the interventional pulmonology wheelhouse. The question is whether bronchoscopically delivered R F A, using a saline microperfusion system, can achieve meaningful local control for small malignant lung tumors while avoiding some of the pleural and parenchymal injury we associate with percutaneous ablation.

Narrator: The clinical rationale is straightforward. Surgery remains the preferred curative approach for operable early lung cancer, and S B R T is an established nonsurgical local therapy. Percutaneous ablation is also used, but pneumothorax, hemoptysis, heat-sink effects, tissue impedance, and access limitations all matter. A transbronchial route is attractive because it follows the airway, potentially reduces pleural puncture, and fits into the navigation bronchoscopy ecosystem.

Guest: The technical innovation here is saline microperfusion. The device, BroncAblate, infuses saline locally to reduce tissue impedance and improve radiofrequency conduction. The system also monitors temperature and impedance at the distal catheter, targeting an ablation-zone temperature between 60 and 90 degrees Celsius. So this is not just a catheter through a bronchoscope. It is a navigation, imaging, thermal-dose, and follow-up imaging workflow.

Narrator: The study was prospective, multicenter, open-label, and single-arm. It enrolled adults with malignant lung tumors across 16 centers in China. Eligible lesions were less than 3 centimeters and accessible through a bronchial path with bronchoscopy and radial E B U S. The study included 126 patients with 130 pulmonary lesions. The median age was 68 years, and most patients were either unsuitable for surgery, had unresectable disease, or declined surgery.

Guest: Most were early-stage. About 82 percent had stage one lung cancer before R F A, and adenocarcinoma was the dominant histology. The lesions were small, with a mean long-axis diameter of 18.77 millimeters and short-axis diameter of 14.15 millimeters. The imaging phenotype is key: about 45 percent were solid nodules, 15 percent pure ground-glass nodules, and 40 percent mixed ground-glass nodules.

Narrator: The primary endpoint was complete ablation at 6 months, based on contrast C T or P E T C T criteria such as nonenhancing or nonmetabolic nodules, cavities, fibrosis, or atelectasis. Secondary endpoints included 12-month complete ablation, overall survival, intrapulmonary progression-free survival, quality of life, technical feasibility, and safety.

Guest: The procedural workflow was advanced. Pathways were planned with virtual navigation bronchoscopy. Patients underwent general anesthesia with muscle relaxation and mechanical ventilation. Catheter positioning was reconfirmed with cone-beam C T before ablation. The figure in the paper is useful because it shows the full sequence: preoperative lesion, cone-beam C T catheter confirmation, immediate post-ablation target zone, and serial C T evolution through 12 months.

Narrator: The technical success rate was very high: 152 of 153 R F A sessions, or 99.35 percent. The median ablation power was 20 watts, the median ablation duration was 19.40 minutes, and the median number of ablations per session was one. The average ablation-zone size at 1 month was 32.47 millimeters, clinically notable given that the target lesions were all under 3 centimeters.

Guest: One nuance is complementary ablation. This was not purely a one-and-done intervention. Complementary ablation was built into the regimen after assessment at 3 months, and 23 complementary ablation attempts occurred, with one unsuccessful catheter placement. That means the efficacy signal reflects a treatment strategy, not necessarily single-session definitive ablation in every case.

Narrator: The efficacy numbers were strong. The 6-month complete ablation rate was 93.75 percent, or 120 of 128 evaluable lesions. At 12 months, complete ablation was 90.48 percent, or 114 of 126 evaluable lesions. One-year overall survival was 96.83 percent, and intrapulmonary progression-free survival was 88.89 percent.

Guest: The subgroup results are the part most people will remember. At 12 months, complete ablation was 82.14 percent for solid nodules, 100 percent for pure ground-glass nodules, and 96.08 percent for mixed ground-glass nodules, with p equals 0.007. Intrapulmonary progression-free survival showed a similar pattern: 78.95 percent for solid nodules, 100 percent for pure ground-glass nodules, and 96.08 percent for mixed ground-glass nodules. The survival curve analysis comparing solid to ground-glass lesions reported a hazard ratio of 5.95, with a 95 percent confidence interval from 2.04 to 17.33, and p equals 0.001.

Narrator: Tumor size trended in the expected direction but was less definitive statistically. Lesions under 2 centimeters had a 12-month complete ablation rate of 93.33 percent, compared with 86.27 percent for 2 to 3 centimeter lesions. Intrapulmonary P F S was 92.95 percent versus 83.63 percent. But the reported comparisons did not clearly meet statistical significance.

Guest: Safety is probably the other major focus. No device defects were reported. The median post-procedure hospital stay was 3 days. The commonly reported respiratory adverse events were pneumothorax in 3.97 percent, hemoptysis in 6.35 percent, pleural effusion in 8.73 percent, pulmonary infection in 11.11 percent, and pleural pain in 10.32 percent. There were no reported cases of hemothorax. Three pneumothoraces and three pleural effusions required drainage and prolonged hospitalization.

Narrator: There was one procedure-related death from massive hemoptysis. That case deserves attention. The patient was a 71-year-old man with compromised lung function and squamous cell carcinoma in the right upper lobe. The lesion was a solid nodule, about 23 by 15.5 millimeters, adjacent to a vessel and cavity. He developed infection after ablation, later hemoptysis, ablation-related cavitary change, and ultimately died despite antibiotic and hemostatic therapy.

Guest: That is the sober reminder. Transbronchial access may reduce some pleural-access complications, but it does not eliminate thermal injury, cavitation, infection, vascular-adjacent bleeding risk, or airway catastrophe. The paper emphasizes vigilance for cavity formation within the first week after R F A and careful evaluation when hemoptysis risk appears to be increasing.

Narrator: Pulmonary function was reassuring in the available follow-up data. Among patients with 12-month pulmonary function results, there was no significant deterioration in F E V one, F E V one to F V C ratio, or D L C O. Quality of life did not show a meaningful global improvement, and fatigue, pain, and dyspnea scores increased slightly, but by less than the 10-point threshold the authors considered clinically significant.

Guest: Now the critical appraisal. The strengths are real. This is prospective, multicenter, and substantially larger than prior transbronchial R F A reports. The authors used frequent follow-up imaging, cone-beam C T confirmation, and third-party imaging assessment for ablation response. For bronchoscopic ablation, this is important evidence.

Narrator: But the limitations are just as important. It is single-arm. There is no direct comparator against S B R T, percutaneous R F A, microwave ablation, or surgery. The enrolled lesions were small, under 3 centimeters, and had to be bronchoscopically accessible. That selects for a very specific procedural anatomy. Also, 13 patients were diagnosed without pathologic confirmation, based on history, imaging growth, and multidisciplinary review.

Guest: And 16 patients continued systemic anticancer therapy during follow-up, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy. That does not invalidate the local ablation results, but it complicates interpretation of progression and survival. One-year overall survival in this mixed population is not the cleanest measure of ablation efficacy.

Narrator: Endpoint selection matters too. Complete ablation on imaging is clinically meaningful, but it is not pathologic eradication. This is especially tricky in ground-glass lesions, where biology is often more indolent, growth kinetics are slower, and post-ablation imaging interpretation can be nuanced. The strong G G N signal may reflect device performance, lesion biology, imaging phenotype, or all of the above.

Guest: For an I P audience, the practical translation is not, “replace established local therapy.” It is more like, “this may be a credible bronchoscopic local therapy strategy for highly selected nonsurgical patients, particularly bronchoscopically accessible G G Ns under 3 centimeters, in centers with navigation, cone-beam C T, ablation expertise, and disciplined imaging follow-up.”

Narrator: For solid tumors, enthusiasm should be more restrained. The 12-month complete ablation rate of 82.14 percent and intrapulmonary P F S of 78.95 percent are promising but not definitive, and the fatal hemoptysis case occurred in a solid, vessel-adjacent, cavitary-risk context. Patient selection will matter: bronchus access, pleural distance, vascular proximity, lesion density, cavitation risk, baseline lung function, and availability of rescue interventions all have to be part of the decision.

Guest: Another workflow point: this is resource intensive. It requires advanced navigation, radial E B U S, cone-beam C T, general anesthesia, a specialized ablation platform, radiology collaboration, and structured surveillance at 24 hours and then 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. That is very different from simply adding a therapeutic catheter to routine diagnostic bronchoscopy.

Narrator: The study also appears device-specific and sponsor-funded, although the authors state that the funder and sponsor had no role in study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, or manuscript writing. That does not negate the data, but it reinforces the need for independent replication, longer follow-up, and comparative trials.

Guest: The bottom line is that Zhong and colleagues provide meaningful prospective evidence that transbronchial R F A with automatic saline microperfusion can be technically feasible and can produce high short-term local control in selected lung tumors, especially pure and mixed G G Ns. The safety profile looks favorable compared with historical concerns around percutaneous ablation, particularly pneumothorax, but serious complications, including fatal hemoptysis, remain possible.

Narrator: So the article adds momentum to bronchoscopic ablation as a therapeutic frontier in I P. It does not settle where this fits relative to S B R T, percutaneous ablation, microwave ablation, or surgery. The next questions are durability beyond one year, performance in solid tumors, reproducibility outside expert centers, cost and workflow feasibility, and whether comparative trials can show a patient-centered advantage.

Guest: That is probably the right takeaway. This is not a practice-ending paper. It is a practice-shaping signal. For I P clinicians building programs around robotic bronchoscopy, cone-beam C T, and procedural oncology, it gives a concrete dataset to discuss with thoracic oncology, radiation oncology, thoracic surgery, and radiology. But it should be applied with humility, careful case selection, and a clear plan for managing post-ablation complications.

Narrator: That wraps up this episode of InterventionalPulm.com I P Journal Review. We reviewed Zhong and colleagues in Respirology, a prospective multicenter study of transbronchial radiofrequency ablation with saline microperfusion for lung tumors. The major message: technically impressive, especially promising for ground-glass lesions, but still limited by single-arm design, selected anatomy, short follow-up, and the need for comparative evidence before broad clinical adoption.
