Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. Today we’re looking at Fernandez-Bussy and colleagues, published in Lung Cancer in 2026, on bronchoscopic pulsed-electric field ablation for pulmonary carcinoid tumors using both robotic-assisted and non-robotic bronchoscopic approaches.

Guest: And this is an interesting paper because it sits right at the intersection of several things interventional pulmonologists are actively thinking about: robotic bronchoscopy moving beyond diagnosis, bronchoscopic ablation as local therapy, and what to do for selected carcinoid patients who are not good surgical or radiation candidates.

Narrator: Right. The authors frame the clinical problem pretty clearly. Pulmonary carcinoids are generally indolent neuroendocrine tumors, and for localized disease, surgery remains the standard treatment. But there is always a subset of patients who are medically inoperable, poor radiation candidates, or who decline more invasive therapy. Traditional endobronchial tools like laser, APC, electrocautery, and cryotherapy can debulk, especially in central airway disease, but they do not necessarily provide controlled tumor eradication, and thermal injury is always part of the calculus.

Guest: That’s the niche this paper is exploring. Pulsed-electric field ablation, or P E F ablation, is non-thermal. The device delivers short-duration, high-voltage electrical pulses through a needle, aiming to induce cell death while theoretically preserving adjacent collagenous structures, airway, and vasculature better than thermal modalities. That’s the appeal, at least mechanistically. But the key question is whether that translates into meaningful local control in a tumor biology like carcinoid.

Narrator: So let’s get into the study design. This was a multicenter retrospective cohort of patients treated between June 2024 and July 2025. Adults were included if they had prior or intraprocedural confirmation of typical carcinoid, were considered unsuitable for surgery or radiation, or declined invasive surgical options. Target lesions had to be under 5 centimeters and at least 10 millimeters from great vessels. Patients needed at least one month of imaging or bronchoscopic follow-up.

Guest: That selection criterion matters. This is not a broad carcinoid treatment trial. It’s a feasibility and safety report in a highly selected group. That’s not a criticism; it’s just the correct frame. The question is not, “Should P E F replace surgery for localized carcinoid?” The question is, “Can this be delivered bronchoscopically in selected patients, with acceptable early safety and some signal of local response?”

Narrator: Procedurally, most lesions were approached with shape-sensing robotic-assisted bronchoscopy, and a smaller number with linear EBUS. The authors used mobile cone-beam CT and 3D fluoroscopy to confirm tool-in-lesion, including vascular delimitations. The figures on pages 2 and 3 are actually useful here: they show the robotic airway reconstruction, target marking, pleural and vascular boundaries, and then the P E F needle position confirmed on intraprocedural CT.

Guest: That visual workflow is important because bronchoscopic ablation is less forgiving than biopsy. With biopsy, near-miss sampling may still produce diagnostic tissue in some circumstances. With ablation, if the energy is not delivered to the right place and with reasonable lesion coverage, the treatment effect is questionable. So the emphasis on tool-in-lesion confirmation, vectors, and repeated imaging is central to the paper.

Narrator: The ablation itself used the Aliya P E F system with a 21-gauge INUMI Flex Needle. For robotic cases, biopsy could be performed first by FNA, forceps, or cryoprobe if diagnostic confirmation was needed, with ROSE available. EBUS-TBNA staging was performed when considered necessary. Each P E F activation lasted five minutes, and the number of activations was left to lesion characteristics and proceduralist preference.

Guest: That last piece is worth pausing on. The number of activations was not protocolized in a way that lets us infer an optimized dose-response relationship. This is early technical experience. It tells us what the operators did, not necessarily what the ideal ablation algorithm should be.

Narrator: In terms of the cohort, the paper reports ten pulmonary carcinoid tumors in eight patients. Median age was 76. All patients were female. Eight peripheral pulmonary nodules from seven patients were treated by robotic bronchoscopy, and two lesions from one patient were treated using linear EBUS. Five patients had P E F ablation alone after prior diagnostic confirmation, three underwent biopsy plus ablation, and two underwent biopsy, ablation, and EBUS-TBNA staging during the same anesthetic event.

Guest: That “single-session” concept is one of the more provocative workflow implications. Diagnosis, staging, and treatment in one anesthetic event is the aspirational model for some bronchoscopic oncology pathways. But it also creates pressure points: ROSE reliability, adequacy of carcinoid classification, confidence that the lesion is typical rather than atypical, and whether definitive treatment should occur before final pathology is available.

Narrator: The lesions were small overall. Median pre-ablation maximum diameter was 13.3 millimeters, all were solid, and nine of the ten were peripheral. One lesion was endobronchial. Median procedure duration was 129 minutes, with an interquartile range of 85 to 177 minutes. The median number of activations was 4, each lasting 5 minutes.

Guest: Those are not trivial procedure times. The authors point out that early cases took longer, with repeated cone-beam CT confirmation, needle repositioning, vector planning, biopsy, and sometimes mediastinal staging. That’s understandable, but from an implementation standpoint, this is not just “add five minutes of ablation to a robotic biopsy.” It requires anesthesia planning, imaging availability, device setup, staff familiarity, and a bleeding-management strategy.

Narrator: Safety is probably the most important early endpoint here. The article reports no pneumothorax and no post-procedural complications within seven days. Length of stay was one day, with overnight observation.

Guest: Bleeding deserves careful reading. The abstract reports three patients with mild intraprocedural bleeding, while the detailed Results section describes two bleeding events: one grade 2 managed with bronchoscope wedging and one grade 3 controlled with Fogarty balloon compression. The Methods define major adverse events as grade 3 or higher bleeding, pneumothorax requiring chest tube, or grade 3 or higher CTCAE events. So there is a little tension in how bleeding severity is framed. The practical message is that bleeding occurred, it was managed bronchoscopically, and there were no downstream reported complications. But carcinoid lesions can be vascular, and teams considering this should not treat bleeding preparedness as optional.

Narrator: Agreed. Now the early efficacy signal: median follow-up was 3.5 months. The median post-ablation maximum diameter was 6.9 millimeters. The paper reports a mean absolute reduction of 8.10 millimeters. The percent reduction is reported as 53.9 percent in the abstract and discussion, with one Results sentence stating 59.3 percent. Either way, the reported range was 25 to 100 percent.

Guest: That is encouraging, but it should be interpreted as an early radiographic response, not definitive oncologic control. For an indolent tumor, three or four months of shrinkage is useful, but it does not answer recurrence, viable residual tumor, progression-free survival, need for repeat ablation, or disease-specific outcomes.

Narrator: Two lesions showed complete resolution at follow-up, one radiographically and one bronchoscopically. The visual examples on pages 6 through 9 show pre- and post-ablation CT and bronchoscopic images, including the endobronchial case with absence of visible tumor at one month. There was also one patient with two untreated nodules that decreased in size, described as a possible abscopal effect, with interval volume reduction of 24.3 percent.

Guest: That’s intriguing, but very hypothesis-generating. In a cohort this small, with carcinoid biology and short follow-up, the abscopal observation is more of a scientific question than a clinical conclusion. It should not be used to imply systemic immune benefit from bronchoscopic P E F in carcinoid. Not yet.

Narrator: Let’s talk about what this adds. The authors state this is, to their knowledge, the first report of robotic-assisted and non-robotic bronchoscopic P E F ablation for carcinoid tumors. That is meaningful because most early endoscopic P E F work has focused on non-small cell lung cancer or metastatic lesions.

Guest: And carcinoid is a different disease. A slow-growing typical carcinoid may make early response look favorable, but it also means long follow-up is essential. Conversely, because many patients live a long time with disease, local control and procedure-related morbidity really matter. A minimally invasive local option could be useful in the right patient, but the bar for claiming durable tumor control is high.

Narrator: The limitations are substantial and the authors acknowledge them. Retrospective design. Small cohort: eight patients, ten lesions. Selection bias. No comparator. Short follow-up, mostly one to four months, with only two cases followed to six to twelve months. And this is a disease where meaningful follow-up is measured in years, not weeks.

Guest: Another limitation is endpoint choice. Size reduction is reasonable for early feasibility, but it is not the same as pathologic complete response. Post-ablation imaging can be difficult to interpret. Scarring, necrosis, inflammation, atelectasis, and granulation tissue can all complicate measurement. For future studies, the field will need standardized imaging response criteria, surveillance intervals, retreatment thresholds, and ideally tissue correlation when feasible.

Narrator: There is also the issue of generalizability. This workflow used advanced bronchoscopy, mobile cone-beam CT, shape-sensing robotic navigation, radial EBUS, ROSE, a dedicated P E F platform, and operators comfortable with both advanced diagnostic and therapeutic bronchoscopy.

Guest: Exactly. This is probably not immediately generalizable to low-resource settings or centers without CBCT-enabled bronchoscopy. And even in advanced centers, training and credentialing will matter. Ablation changes the risk conversation. The operator is no longer just obtaining tissue; the operator is delivering local therapy near airways and vessels.

Narrator: So how should an IP audience apply this?

Guest: Carefully. The paper supports feasibility and an early safety signal in selected nonsurgical or nonradiation carcinoid patients. It does not establish P E F as equivalent to surgery, nor does it define durable local control. For now, the most appropriate clinical frame is multidisciplinary consideration for highly selected patients: typical carcinoid, localized disease, poor surgical or radiation candidacy or patient refusal, anatomically accessible lesion, careful vascular assessment, and a team ready to manage bleeding.

Narrator: And the practical procedural takeaway?

Guest: Plan it like a therapeutic case, not like a diagnostic biopsy with an add-on. Confirm tool-in-lesion. Map vessels. Think through airway control, ventilation, CBCT workflow, needle trajectory, number of activations, balloon availability, and post-procedure observation. Also, be cautious when relying on intraprocedural pathology. If ROSE is equivocal, the safer move may be to wait for final pathology rather than ablate first and ask questions later.

Narrator: Final synthesis: Fernandez-Bussy and colleagues provide an early, technically detailed look at bronchoscopic P E F ablation for pulmonary carcinoid tumors. In eight patients with ten treated lesions, the approach was deliverable through robotic bronchoscopy and linear EBUS, with short-term tumor size reduction and no reported pneumothorax or delayed seven-day complications. The safety signal is encouraging, but bleeding reporting requires close attention, and the efficacy signal remains preliminary.

Guest: Right. This paper does not change the standard of care for operable localized pulmonary carcinoid. But it does expand the conversation about bronchoscopic local therapy for patients who lack good conventional options. The next step is not enthusiasm alone; it’s prospective data, longer follow-up, standardized response assessment, clearer ablation parameters, and ideally comparisons against surveillance, radiation, surgery, or other bronchoscopic and percutaneous modalities in appropriately selected patients.

The take-home message: feasible, technically compelling, and promising in the short term, but still early evidence that should be applied with multidisciplinary caution and rigorous follow-up.
