Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we are discussing Benn and colleagues, published in CHEST Pulmonary in 2025, on indocyanine green-soaked fiducial markers for localizing lung nodules before thoracic surgery. The clinical question is practical: can a bronchoscopically placed, fluorescent fiducial help surgeons resect small peripheral pulmonary lesions while preserving more lung parenchyma?
Guest: Right, and that question matters because the field has moved toward more lung-sparing resections for carefully selected early-stage lung cancers, especially after the sublobar resection data from JCOG0802 and CALGB 140503. As the target lesions get smaller, deeper, or more ground-glass in character, localization becomes less of a convenience issue and more of an oncologic workflow issue. If a lesion cannot be found confidently, the operation may become larger than intended.
Narrator: This was a retrospective, multicenter review across four institutions. The investigators compared 54 nodules marked with ICG-soaked fiducials, or ICG-F, with 63 unmarked nodules from a historical control period immediately before the technique was adopted. The primary procedural question was whether marking changed the surgical specimen size and weight.
Guest: The headline result was a much smaller resected specimen in the marked group. Mean specimen weight fell from 145 grams in the unmarked cohort to 58 grams in the ICG-F cohort, with P < .001. The physical specimen dimensions were also smaller across all three axes. In subgroup analyses, the same general pattern appeared in both segmentectomies and wedge resections: segmentectomy specimens weighed 24 grams in the marked group versus 162 grams in the unmarked group, and wedge specimens weighed 12 grams versus 23 grams, with the wedge comparison right at P = .05.
Narrator: They also reported 100% intraoperative visualization of marked nodules using near-infrared imaging, and the markers remained effective for as long as 13 days after placement. That durability is clinically important because it separates the localization procedure from the surgical schedule. A marker that still works days later is much more usable than a dye injection that demands immediate surgery.
Guest: That is the strongest practical argument for the technique. It combines two useful properties: a fiducial provides a physical target, and ICG provides a fluorescent signal that the surgeon can see. But the methodological limits are substantial. This was not randomized, and the comparator was a historical unmarked group. That creates a real risk that changes in surgeon behavior, institutional workflow, case selection, or technology adoption over time contributed to the observed difference.
Narrator: The baseline nodule differences are central. The ICG-F nodules were smaller at baseline, with a mean axial diameter around 14 mm versus 20 mm in the unmarked group. They also had a bronchus sign more often, 81% versus 63%. That is a little counterintuitive if one expects marked lesions to be harder to reach, but it also suggests the two cohorts were not just interchangeable versions of the same population.
Guest: Exactly. This is classic confounding by indication. Smaller or less palpable nodules may have been selected for marking precisely because the procedural team anticipated localization difficulty. The authors make a reasonable counterargument: one might expect a surgeon to take more tissue for a smaller, harder-to-find unmarked lesion to make sure it is captured. But the study design cannot fully separate the effect of the marker from the clinical judgment that led to marking in the first place.
Narrator: Another issue is the endpoint. Smaller specimen weight is attractive, but it is still a surrogate. The real oncologic endpoint is adequate margin with durable local control. The study is summarized as reporting that margins were at least 1 cm for all resected specimens, but the granularity is limited. Without detailed margin distances, margin-to-tumor ratios, local recurrence, or longer-term cancer outcomes, smaller is not automatically better.
Guest: That is the key devil's advocate point. Parenchymal preservation is valuable only if oncologic clearance is not compromised. The paper supports technical localization and suggests a tissue-sparing effect, but it does not prove superior cancer care. It is also notable that operative time was longer in the marked cohort: 165 minutes versus 136 minutes. That 29-minute difference was statistically significant, and it matters in a procedural environment where operating room time, anesthesia time, and throughput are finite resources.
Narrator: The longer operative time is interesting because one might assume localization should make the case faster. The authors suggest that these were smaller or more complex lesions and that meticulous dissection or a learning curve may explain the difference. There was no difference in chest tube duration or length of stay, which helps place the time difference in context. The report also notes one unmarked case converted from wedge to lobectomy because the nodule could not be localized, whereas that did not occur in the marked group.
Guest: Avoiding an unintended larger resection is a meaningful benefit, even if it is hard to quantify in a small retrospective cohort. But implementation is not trivial. This is a two-step pathway: bronchoscopy with marker placement followed by surgery, sometimes days later. That creates scheduling flexibility, but it also requires coordination between IP, thoracic surgery, anesthesia, radiology, perioperative nursing, and the patient. If the system is not built for that handoff, the technique may be technically elegant but operationally difficult.
Narrator: Generalizability also deserves attention. The study involved centers with advanced localization capabilities. Shape-sensing robotic bronchoscopy was used in many cases, and cone-beam CT confirmation was used in many. In centers without robotic bronchoscopy, CBCT, or near-infrared surgical imaging, the same localization performance may not be achievable. The bronchoscopic diagnostic yield preceding marker placement was 78%, which is respectable, but it also means that diagnosis was not secured bronchoscopically in about one fifth of cases before proceeding toward surgery.
Guest: The comparator is another limitation. This was ICG-F versus no localization, not versus pleural dye, percutaneous wire or fiducial placement, microcoil localization, or CBCT-guided resection without a physical marker. So the paper supports feasibility and suggests benefit compared with no marker, but it does not define where this technique fits relative to other localization strategies.
Narrator: The practical synthesis is that ICG-F appears to be a technically reliable way to localize selected peripheral pulmonary lesions for surgery, with excellent reported intraoperative visualization and useful durability over several days. It may help surgeons perform smaller resections for lesions that would otherwise be difficult to find.
Guest: But the confidence level should remain measured. The study is retrospective, the cohorts differ at baseline, the control group is historical, operative time was longer, and oncologic outcomes are not available. For now, this is best viewed as a strong feasibility and workflow study, not definitive evidence that ICG-F improves patient-centered or cancer-specific outcomes.
Narrator: For an IP audience, the take-home is to think of this as a potentially valuable localization tool for carefully selected small, deep, or subsolid nodules, especially in programs with robotic bronchoscopy, CBCT confirmation, and thoracic surgeons using near-infrared imaging. The next step would be prospective comparative data with margin quality, conversion rates, operative workflow, complications, cost, and recurrence.
