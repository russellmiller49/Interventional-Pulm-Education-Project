Narrator: Welcome to InterventionalPulm.com IP Journal Review. In this episode, we’re looking at Kontogianni and colleagues, published in the Journal of Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology in 2026. The paper is titled, “Global Survey on Current Practices of Endobronchial Ultrasound Approaches to N Two Staging in Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer Following the Ninth Edition of the T N M Classification.”

Guest: And this is a very practical paper. It is not a trial of EBUS technique, and it does not prove that one staging workflow is superior. But it gets at a real procedural question created by T N M nine: if N two disease is now subdivided into single-station N two A and multi-station N two B, should we be changing the EBUS needle between different N two nodal stations to avoid cross-contamination and false upstaging?

Narrator: Right. Historically, N two was largely an anatomic category. Now, the number of involved mediastinal stations matters. The paper emphasizes that single-site N two disease carries a better prognosis than multi-site N two disease, with cited overall survival estimates of 47 percent for N two A versus 36 percent for N two B. That distinction can affect stage grouping, prognosis, multidisciplinary discussions, and potentially radiation fields.

Guest: Which means the EBUS operator is no longer just asking, “Is there N two disease?” The question becomes, “Is this truly one N two station or more than one?” That sounds subtle, but in the procedure suite it creates an awkward question: after sampling one positive N two node, can the same needle be used to sample another N two station without risking tumor cell carryover?

Narrator: The authors designed a short, 10-question online survey, distributed from March 4 through March 16, 2025, through interventional pulmonology networks, society channels, email lists, and social media. They analyzed 605 questionnaires from 66 countries. Most respondents worked in academic or university hospitals, and most reported doing EBUS T B N A regularly. Nearly 29 percent performed 1 to 2 procedures weekly, and about 14 percent performed more than 10 per week.

Guest: So, this is not a survey of occasional bronchoscopists only. It appears enriched for engaged, society-connected, academically oriented operators. That is a strength if the goal is to understand what experienced IP clinicians are thinking. But it is also a limitation, because this is not a denominator-based global sampling frame. We don’t know the response rate, and the results may not generalize to every setting where lung cancer staging happens.

Narrator: The headline result is the gap between belief and behavior. When asked whether they actually change the needle between punctures of two N two lymph nodes, only 5.5 percent said yes. About 57 percent said no, and about 10 percent said sometimes. But when asked whether they believe it is important to change the needle between two N two nodes, 20.7 percent said yes, and 17.4 percent said sometimes.

Guest: That’s the key tension. Roughly 38 percent thought needle exchange was at least sometimes important, but only a small fraction actually reported doing it. Among those who considered needle exchange advisable at least sometimes, the major barrier was cost, cited by 68.3 percent. Lack of evidence was cited by 31.3 percent. That is a very familiar IP problem: the biologic concern may be plausible, but the operational cost is immediate and very real.

Narrator: The free-text responses are where the paper becomes especially interesting. Respondents against needle change generally made several arguments. One was that a systematic staging sequence should be sufficient: sample from least likely to most likely involved. Another was that flushing the needle should be enough. Others pointed to lack of evidence, cost, waste, and local standards. And some comments suggested incomplete awareness or implementation of T N M nine, including the idea that “all N twos are in the same stage.”

Guest: That last point matters. If an operator does not recognize that N two A and N two B are now separate descriptors with staging and prognostic consequences, then the procedural concern will seem artificial. But once that distinction is accepted, the question becomes harder to dismiss.

Narrator: On the other side, respondents who favored needle exchange focused on avoiding false multi-station N two classification. In other words, the concern is not just cytologic tidiness. It is that contaminating a second N two station could move a patient from N two A to N two B, with downstream implications for staging and treatment planning.

Guest: And the “sometimes” group may be closest to how many clinicians actually think. They suggested tailoring needle exchange to the situation: suspicious ultrasound morphology, P E T C T findings concerning for multiple N two stations, positive ROSE in one station before sampling another, or cases where the distinction between stages would change treatment intent.

Narrator: The paper also reports P E T C T availability before bronchoscopy. About 12 percent said always, nearly 40 percent said most of the time, about 20 percent said rarely, and about 4 percent said never. A quarter did not answer. So P E T C T is often available, but not universally, and that matters if one proposed solution is to use imaging to decide which stations merit separate needles.

Guest: ROSE is similar, and maybe even more complicated. Several respondents suggested changing the needle if ROSE confirms malignancy in one N two station and another N two station still needs sampling. That is pragmatic. But the survey did not explicitly assess ROSE availability, so it cannot tell us whether that strategy is feasible globally. In many settings, ROSE is not available, inconsistent, or limited by staffing.

Narrator: The paper does not ignore the biologic plausibility of contamination. The authors cite prior work by Puyal and colleagues, where cytologic contamination persisted in 43.5 percent of cases despite multiple saline flushes. They also mention a 2017 ATS poster reporting needle contamination despite flushing. That supports the idea that “just flush the needle” may not fully solve the problem.

Guest: But this is where the devil’s advocate position is important. Cytologic contamination of the needle is not the same as clinically meaningful false upstaging. The evidence would need to show that carryover cells actually produce a false diagnosis in a second nodal station at a rate high enough to matter, and that needle exchange prevents that outcome in a cost-effective way. That evidence is not established here.

Narrator: Exactly. This study maps uncertainty. It does not resolve the uncertainty. It tells us that the IP community is divided, that real-world practice lags behind theoretical concern, and that cost and lack of evidence are major barriers.

Guest: Methodologically, the survey has the expected limitations. Distribution was informal, so there is no response rate. Duplicate responses cannot be fully excluded. The survey was targeted toward interventional pulmonologists and not broadly distributed through thoracic surgical societies or the ACCP, so it may miss important perspectives. There were also substantial missing responses for some questions. And because the questionnaire was intentionally brief, it could not capture important local variables like needle type, number of passes, use of suction, specimen handling, ROSE availability, sedation model, cytology processing workflow, or institutional staging algorithms.

Narrator: The figures reinforce the message. The world map in the article shows broad geographic participation, but it should not be interpreted as population-level representativeness. And the bar charts on needle management are probably the cleanest visual summary of the paper: actual practice is heavily weighted toward not changing needles, while belief about whether needle change matters is more mixed.

Guest: Clinically, the immediate takeaway is not, “Everyone must change needles between N two stations tomorrow.” That would overstate the evidence. A more reasonable takeaway is that T N M nine forces IP teams to revisit their mediastinal staging workflow. The traditional systematic approach still matters, but now the order of sampling, interpretation of ROSE, and handling of separate N two stations may require more explicit local protocols.

Narrator: A practical staging discussion might now include several questions before the case starts. Which nodes are suspicious by P E T C T, C T, and EBUS morphology? Which nodal stations, if positive, would alter stage group or treatment pathway? If ROSE is available and the first N two station is malignant, will a second N two station be sampled with a new needle? If ROSE is unavailable, is there a default sequence that minimizes contamination risk while preserving diagnostic completeness?

Guest: And the cost conversation should be honest. Changing needles between every station would increase procedural cost and waste. In some regions, even single-use needle availability is constrained. So any proposed guideline has to be implementable outside high-resource centers. Otherwise, it becomes an expert-center recommendation masquerading as a global standard.

Narrator: The authors ultimately call for more research and suggest that a joint expert statement from groups such as ERS, EABIP, WABIP, EACTS, and ATS would be useful. That seems reasonable. But before consensus becomes prescriptive, the field needs better data.

Guest: The ideal study would probably be prospective and procedural. It would standardize nodal sampling order, track ROSE status, evaluate needle contamination after each station, and correlate suspected carryover with final cytology, surgical pathology when available, imaging follow-up, and perhaps molecular profiling to distinguish true nodal involvement from contamination. It would also need a cost-effectiveness component, because this entire debate hinges on whether a rare but consequential error justifies routine needle exchange.

Narrator: So, final synthesis: Kontogianni and colleagues give us a timely snapshot of global IP thinking after T N M nine. The study shows wide variation in attitudes toward EBUS needle exchange between N two stations, a major gap between theoretical concern and real-world practice, and persistent uncertainty about how to operationalize N two A versus N two B staging.

Guest: For the IP audience, the most useful response is not panic and not dismissal. It is to recognize that multi-station N two disease now carries more formal staging weight, that needle contamination is biologically plausible, that flushing may not completely eliminate contamination, but that the clinical impact remains unproven. Until better evidence or consensus guidance is available, the best approach is deliberate staging strategy, careful sampling sequence, thoughtful use of P E T C T and ROSE when available, and clear multidisciplinary communication about uncertainty.
