Physics & Practice of Hot Ablation for Central Airway Disease

A hands-on primer on the thermal modalities that produce immediate airway patency and hemostasis — medical lasers, electrocautery/diathermy, and argon plasma coagulation — plus how they contrast with the delayed “cold” therapies. Manipulate the physics, then apply it to real lesions.

1Overview

Why “hot,” and why now?

Central airway obstruction (CAO) arises from intrinsic endoluminal tumor, extrinsic compression, or mixed disease. Thermal modalities treat the endoluminal component and deliver their effect in real time — the reason they are chosen when a patient needs the lumen open now (critical stenosis, stridor) or bleeding stopped now (endobronchial hemoptysis).

Immediate effect

Laser · Electrocautery · APC vaporize, cut, and coagulate as you fire — patency and hemostasis are achieved during the case.

Delayed effect

Cryoablation · PDT · Brachytherapy work over hours to weeks. Excellent adjuncts, but not for the crashing airway.

Coagulate → debulk

The unifying principle: devascularize first at low power density, then remove tissue — mechanically or by vaporization — in a multimodal sequence.

2Laser Physics

Light, and what tissue does with it

A laser is coherent light with three defining properties, and every clinical effect follows from how that light meets tissue.

Monochromatic

A single wavelength → a single, predictable absorption behavior in tissue. Wavelength is the master variable.

Coherent

Waves in phase, in space and time → energy stays concentrated instead of dispersing.

Collimated

A tight, parallel beam that can be focused to a small spot → very high power density on target.

Laser–tissue interaction — click a phenomenon

When the beam hits tissue, incident energy is divided four ways. The balance between them — set by wavelength and tissue optics — decides whether you get a shallow cut or deep coagulation.

The through-line

Only absorbed energy heats tissue. A wavelength that is poorly absorbed (Nd:YAG, 1064 nm) scatters and transmits deep before it is absorbed → deep, broad coagulation. A highly absorbed wavelength (CO₂, Ho:YAG, 1470 nm) dumps its energy in the first <1 mm → precise, shallow cutting with minimal collateral.

5Waveforms

Duty cycle & crest factor — the "wavelength" of electrosurgery

Every mode is the same ~350 kHz sine carrier, packaged differently. Two numbers describe the package: duty cycle (what fraction of time the generator is actually on) and crest factor (peak voltage ÷ RMS — how "spiky" the waveform is). Continuous low-crest waveforms cut; interrupted high-crest, high-voltage bursts coagulate.

CUT — continuous

100% duty · crest ≈1.6 · modest Vp
  • Steady arc → continuous vaporization
  • Least hemostasis, least collateral
  • autoCUT · highCUT

BLEND-like — modulated cut

~17% duty · crest ≈3.4 · higher Vp
  • Cuts, but tissue cools between packets
  • Pronounced hemostatic margin
  • dryCUT (and classic "blend")

COAG — high-V bursts

~3–6% duty · crest 5.8–7.7 · up to 4300 Vp
  • Sparks fulgurate / desiccate the surface
  • High voltage → coupling & char risk
  • forced · swift · sprayCOAG
The softCOAG exception — learn it

softCOAG breaks the "coag = high voltage" rule on purpose. It is a continuous sine capped at 200 V — below the ~200 V arcing threshold — so it never sparks. No spark → no surface char → energy keeps conducting into tissue → the deepest coagulation of any mode, with far less electrode adhesion. Low voltage, deep effect.

Why crest factor matters for safety

Coag modes reach 1800–4300 V peak (vs 750 V for autoCUT). Higher peaks arc farther, char more, and drive capacitive coupling through insulation in minimally-invasive work. Use the lowest mode/effect that achieves the goal.

Effect vs watts

Effect (0.1–10) is the dial: the VIO regulates peak voltage / arc intensity to hold it constant as tissue impedance changes. The wattage is a ceiling the generator may use, not a command. Raise Effect for more tissue effect — not the power limit.

AUTO STOP & max activation

softCOAG's AUTO STOP ends activation when desiccation is sufficient (try it — hold the COAG pedal on softCOAG). The unit also monitors maximum activation time; the sim auto-releases at 5 s. Short, deliberate activations are the habit to build.

NESSY blocks unsafe monopolar

7Current Density

Same watts, opposite effects

Hold power constant and change only the electrode contact area. A fine needle concentrates current into a tiny cross-section — density soars, water flashes to steam, tissue cuts. A broad ball or forceps jaw spreads the same current — density falls, tissue slowly desiccates. This is also why the return pad never burns when properly applied: it's just a very, very large electrode.

Halving the contact diameter roughly quadruples current density in tissue. The generator setting didn't change — your instrument choice and pressure did. Snare wire cutting through a stalk is a moving needle; forceps jaws grabbing a vessel are a ball.

The pad is the punchline

The dispersive return electrode is ~130 cm² of the same circuit. Full, even contact → negligible density → no heat. Peel half of it off and the remaining edge becomes a small electrode on skin — that is the pad-burn mechanism NESSY exists to catch.

8Circuit

Monopolar vs bipolar — where does the current go?

Monopolar delivery sends current from the instrument tip through the patient to a dispersive return pad; the whole torso is part of your circuit. Bipolar confines the loop to the millimeters between two jaws — no pad, minimal stray current, the CIED-friendly and MIS-friendly choice when it can do the job.

Monopolar: tip → through the torso → dispersive pad → back to the generator.
Pad placement rules

Full contact on clean, dry, shaved, well-perfused muscle (thigh/flank), cable-side toward the surgical field, not over bone, scars, implants, or ECG electrodes, and positioned so the current path avoids the heart and any CIED. Re-check after repositioning the patient.

Coupling injuries (MIS)

Insulation failure, direct coupling (touching another instrument), and capacitive coupling through an intact sheath all divert current to tissue you are not looking at. High-voltage COAG modes are the worst offenders — keep the tip in view, activate only on target, and use the lowest effective voltage mode.

10Modalities

Laser vs electrocautery vs APC — and the cold options

Electrocautery / diathermy

Contact delivery of high-frequency current → resistive heating. Cut = low voltage / high current (vaporizing arc); coag = high voltage / low current (desiccation); blend splits the difference. Tools: needle knife, snare, hot forceps, blunt probe. Electrical → manage pacers/ICDs.

Drive the simulated ERBE VIO 3 console (modes, APC, waveforms & tissue lab) ↑

Argon plasma coagulation

Non-contact monopolar current arced through ionized argon gas. Superficial 1–3 mm effect that flows around corners — the hemostasis workhorse for diffuse oozing. Keep gas flow ≤1.0 L/min; don’t fire while advancing (gas-embolism risk).

Laser

Coherent light; contact (bare fiber) or non-contact. Deepest coagulation (Nd:YAG) and effective vaporization → rapid debulking of intrinsic tumors when a distal lumen is visible. Light, not current → safe around CIEDs.

Why choose “hot” — the cold contrast

Cryotherapy (freeze), photodynamic therapy, and brachytherapy are potent but delayed — necrosis develops over hours to weeks and often needs a clean-up bronchoscopy. They’re wrong for the crashing airway. The exception: cryo-adhesion (cryoextraction) pulls clot, tumor, or foreign bodies out mechanically and immediately — and carries no fire risk, useful when FiO₂ can’t be lowered.

11Safety

Airway fire & periprocedural safety

Safety is core content, not a footnote. An airway fire needs three things at once — remove any one and there is no fire.

The fire triangle — click a leg to control it
All three present → fire is possible. Control any one leg.

Pre-laser “time-out” checklist
    Eyewear is wavelength-specific

    Goggles are filters tuned to a wavelength — not interchangeable. Nd:YAG eyewear does not protect against KTP. Everyone in the nominal hazard zone wears the correct filter; post door signage; keep the laser in standby until firing.

    Surgical plume

    Vaporization plume carries viable cells, viral DNA, and carcinogens. Run smoke/plume evacuation and wear high-filtration masks — the aerosol is a genuine occupational hazard.

    Gas / air embolism

    Mostly an APC risk: keep gas flow <1.0 L/min, tip off the mucosa, and never fire while advancing or impaling tissue. Laser coaxial coolant gas can embolize too. Sudden CV collapse → stop, support, position.

    Know when NOT to fire

    Extrinsic compression alone → no endoluminal target: dilate ± stent, don’t ablate. Near-complete obstruction with no visualized distal lumen → blind energy risks perforation; establish the lumen first.

    If a fire happens

    Stop the gas and the energy · remove burning material (the ETT) · flood the airway with saline · then re-scope to assess injury and secure the airway. Rehearse it as a team before you start.

    12Safety

    Electrosurgical safety — beyond the fire triangle

    Everything from the airway-fire module still applies — FiO₂/FeO₂ ≤ 0.40 before any energy, wet field, no alcohol prep. Electrosurgery adds a current-specific problem list:

    Return-electrode burns

    The classic electrosurgical injury. Prevention is full-contact pad application + NESSY split-pad impedance monitoring, which blocks monopolar output if contact degrades. An alarm mid-case means stop and fix the pad — never silence it or crank the power. Neonates get dedicated monitoring thresholds.

    CIEDs — pacemakers & ICDs

    RF current can inhibit pacing or trigger shocks. Prefer bipolar or short (<5 s) monopolar bursts, keep the pad so the current vector avoids the generator–lead axis, magnet/asynchronous mode per your device team, and interrogate after. (Lasers sidestep this entirely — light, not current.)

    Surgical smoke

    Electrosurgical plume carries ultrafine particles, carcinogens, and viable biological material. Run plume evacuation and wear high-filtration masks — same discipline as the laser room.

    Scope & field discipline

    Keep the active tip beyond the endoscope (protect optics), in view, and off everything you don't intend to treat. A wet, blood-pooled field blunts the effect — suction first, then activate. In the colon, remember combustible bowel gas; in the airway, oxygen.

    The five-question pre-activation habit

    O₂ ≤ 40%? Pad green (NESSY)? CIED plan? Tip in view & beyond scope? Right mode/effect for the goal? — five seconds, every activation.
